\title{Feature Normalization under Finite Training Budgets}
\altauthor{\Name{Anonymous Authors}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  Standardizing a rare feature can help stochastic gradient descent learn its
  effect within a finite budget, but it also changes the noise cost of learning.
  We characterize the lowest rare-subgroup risks attainable by standardized and
  unscaled training when each chooses any nonnegative constant learning rate and
  deterministic stop under the same observation cap. For two independent binary
  features, we derive both optimized risks as prevalence vanishes while the
  expected number of rare observations remains finite. Uniform control over
  rates and stops reduces each limiting oracle to a scalar minimization. At
  positive noise, the formulas yield a unique crossing as signal moves to the
  rare feature at fixed positive initial limiting subgroup risk, and explain
  how noise and budget change the preferred scaling. Separate finite-prevalence
  bounds favor standardization on a rare-dominant signal family and unscaled
  training on a common-dominant family. The results concern final iterates
  with population preprocessing and fresh observations. Exact-moment searches
  assess the approximation at finite prevalence. In a controlled study, both
  orderings persist under observation reuse, estimated preprocessing, and
  intercept fitting when tuning choices are fixed before evaluation. Selecting a
  predictor after observing training can reverse an ordering, even when
  selection uses exact population risk.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
  Feature normalization, stochastic gradient descent, early stopping,
  rare-subgroup risk
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

A rare feature gives a model few opportunities to learn its effect. Without
scaling, gradient descent may fit common features while leaving the rare feature
largely unlearned. Dividing by the feature's standard deviation increases the
influence of those few observations. This can help recover a useful rare signal,
but it also makes the fitted coefficient more sensitive to noise.

These effects can substantially change prediction where the rare feature is
present, despite contributing little to average error. Normalization therefore
raises a statistical question: how strongly should training respond to scarce
evidence? Scaling for instance leaves a full-rank least-squares solution
unchanged, but changes which signals we learn before stopping.
\Cref{fig:intro-coefficient-paths} illustrates this difference on a fixed
least-squares problem: the methods approach the same solution along different
paths and reach different coefficients when stopped early.

Adjusting the learning rate and stopping time is a natural way to compensate for
different learning speeds. We ask how low each scaling can drive rare-subgroup
risk when both optimize these choices under the same observation cap. Our main
result characterizes both optimized risks in a rare-prevalence limit and
identifies when each scaling is preferable. \Cref{fig:intro-signal-crossing}
illustrates the resulting unique crossing: standardization gives lower risk
when the rare signal dominates, whereas unscaled training gives lower risk when
the common signal dominates. The comparison holds initial subgroup risk,
rescaled budget, and noise level fixed. A separate finite-prevalence theorem
establishes positive gaps for two signal families, illustrated across
observation caps in \cref{fig:conference-tuned-intro}.

We study two independent binary predictors under squared loss, with known
population centering and scales, zero initialization, a fixed zero intercept, and one fresh
observation per update. Our main characterization,
\cref{thm:oracle-limits}, gives both optimized total risks as rare prevalence
$q \to 0$ and the observation cap $M$ satisfies
$Mq(1 - q) \to \tau \in (0,\infty)$, for arbitrary fixed signal strengths and
noise. Uniform bounds over all nonnegative constant rates and deterministic
stops justify passing from the finite-budget oracle infima to scalar limiting
minimizations. These bounds include prevalence-dependent choices, unstable
rates, and vanishing rates that learn the common signal while leaving the rare
effect unchanged. At positive noise and fixed positive initial limiting subgroup
risk, the formulas give exactly one crossing as signal moves to the rare
feature. They also explain how noise and budget can reverse the ordering.

The finite-prevalence comparison in \cref{thm:conference-predictive} complements
this characterization with explicit positive gaps for two signal families.
The gaps hold whenever $0 < q \leq 0.04$ and $1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$,
after each method optimizes over all nonnegative constant rates and
deterministic stops. As prevalence decreases, the budget grows while the
expected number of rare occurrences remains roughly between one and four.
Relative feature scaling therefore remains consequential after tuning as the
total observation cap grows.

The analysis explains this result through the noise cost of learning each
signal. To learn a dominant rare signal, unscaled training also introduces noise
in the common coefficient. To learn a dominant common signal, standardization
introduces noise in the rare coefficient. Exact moments separate these costs
from incomplete learning and variation due to feature sampling.

Exact-moment searches assess the limiting predictions at finite prevalence.
An extension with several common features retains the two-moment reduction and
both numerical orderings through 64 common features.

A controlled study then asks how these comparisons change when training reuses
observations, estimates preprocessing, or fits an intercept. We retain the two
illustrative signal pairs and find that both orderings persist when each
method's tuning choices are fixed before evaluation. Selecting a predictor after
observing training can reverse an ordering, even with exact population risk. We
also examine how missing rare validation observations affect selection.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}

Preconditioning changes statistical behavior as well as learning speed. Its
effect on early-stopped predictions can persist even when the least-squares
optimum is unchanged \citep{wadia2021whitening}. Its benefit can depend on which
features carry the signal, even after tuning the stopping time.
\citet{amari2020preconditioning} establish this dependence for aggregate
prediction bias. \citet{su2024preconditioning} study preconditioning for
fresh-sample SGD, comparing the aggregate excess risk of tail-averaged iterates
with ridge regression.

Evaluating prediction within a rare subgroup also changes how coefficient errors
contribute to risk. Conditioning the test law on rare presence while retaining
the training law connects our objective to work on target-distribution risk and
covariate shift \citep{lei2021shift, wu2022pretraining, liu2025precondition}. We
ask which normalization achieves lower rare-subgroup risk after each method
chooses its nonnegative constant learning rate and deterministic stopping time
under the same observation cap. For final iterates of fresh-sample SGD, our
limiting theorem characterizes the optimized total risk for arbitrary fixed
signals and noise, with uniform control over the full rate-and-stop domain.
Our finite-prevalence theorem establishes opposite positive gaps on two fixed
signal families.

Our calculations build on established mechanisms. Early stopping changes
least-squares regularization \citep{ali2019early}. \citet{ali2020stochastic}
compare stochastic gradient flow with ridge along the training path for a fixed
design. Their Lemma 1 matches discrete SGD's first two moments to an Euler
discretization of the flow; the continuous process is an approximation to SGD.
Analyses of least-mean-squares methods separate bias and variance while
accounting for sampling and batching \citep{defossez2015averaged,
jain2018parallelizing}. AdaBatch connects sparse mini-batch aggregation with
coordinate rescaling \citep{defossez2017adabatch}, while moment operators
characterize exact mean-square stability \citep{mulayoff2024exact}. For explicit
regularization, \citet{larsson2025normalization} analyze how feature scaling
affects shrinkage.

The risks of scaling rare features also have precedents.
\citet[Section 5.4]{bordes2010erratum} warn that standardizing very rare
features can hurt test performance. In a study of deterministic graph neural
network training, \citet[Section 5]{milligan2024normalization} identify
increased stochastic variance as a possible cost of standardizing rare binary
features. These observations motivate quantitative risk comparisons but do not
establish our oracle inequalities.

\section{Normalization, learning, and rare-subgroup risk}
\label{sec:conference-setting}

We isolate the effect of prevalence with two independent binary features: a
common feature $I$ with $\Prob(I = 1) = 1/2$ and a rare feature $J$ with
$\Prob(J = 1) = q$, where $0 < q < 1/2$. For the centered vector
$x = (I - 1/2,J - q)^\top$, suppose
\[
  y = x^\top\beta^* + \varepsilon,\qquad \E(\varepsilon \mid x)
    = 0,\qquad \E(\varepsilon^2 \mid x)
    = \sigma^2.
\]
Write $a = 1/4$, $b = q(1 - q)$, and $C = \E(xx^\top) = \diag(a,b)$. We use
population centering, fix the centered intercept at zero, and initialize
$\beta_0 = 0$.

\paragraph{Scaling and the training budget.}

We divide each feature by a positive scale factor, collected in the diagonal
matrix $S$. Training on $z = S^{-1}x$ with parameter $\theta = S\beta$ gives the
following SGD update on the original coefficient scale:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{k+1}
  = \beta_k
    - \eta S^{-2}x_k(x_k^\top\beta_k - y_k). \label{eq:conference-update}
\end{equation}
We use squared loss with a factor $1/2$. Each update uses one independent fresh
observation, so an observation cap $M$ permits at most $M$ updates. We compare
unscaled features (U), with $P_U = I_2$, and standard-deviation scaling (S, or
SD), with $P_S = \diag(a^{-1},b^{-1})$, where $P = S^{-2}$. Both procedures use
the final iterate.

We can compensate exactly for global rescaling by adjusting the learning rate.
Replacing $S$ by $gS$ and $\eta$ by $g^2\eta$ leaves every update unchanged when
the methods use the same data stream. Changing the relative feature scales,
however, generally changes $\eta S^{-2}$ in a way that a single learning rate
cannot undo. Whether this leads to a risk difference after tuning requires a
separate comparison.

\paragraph{Prediction where the rare feature is present.}

Our main objective is excess prediction risk where the rare feature is present,
$J = 1$. Under half squared loss, independence gives a weighted sum of the two
coefficient mean squared errors:
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k}
  = \frac18\E\bigl[(\beta_{C,k} - \beta_C^*)^2\bigr]
    + \frac{(1 - q)^2}{2}\E\bigl[(\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2\bigr]. \label{eq:conference-rare-risk}
\end{equation}
The expectations average over training randomness, and the risk excludes
irreducible test noise. Aggregate excess risk $R_k$ replaces $(1 - q)^2$ by
$q(1 - q)$ in this expression. Thus aggregate error can give little weight to an
error that matters within the rare subgroup. We evaluate both objectives on the
same trained predictor and retain the original training distribution.

\paragraph{Learning signal and fitting noise.}

Let $e_k = \E(\beta_k - \beta^*)$ and
$V_k = \E\bigl((\beta_k - \beta^*)(\beta_k - \beta^*)^\top\bigr)$. Under fresh
sampling, the error second moment decomposes as
\begin{equation}
  V_k = e_ke_k^\top + A_k + \sigma^2N_k, \label{eq:conference-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $A_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance of the noiseless process under random
feature sampling and $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance contributed by
observation noise. This separates incomplete learning from the two sources of
training variation. \Cref{sec:variance-decomposition} derives the decomposition
and its moment formula.

The mean error obeys $e_{k+1,j} = (1 - \eta C_{jj}/s_j^2)e_{k,j}$. SD scaling
therefore gives every coordinate the multiplier $1 - \eta$. For
$0 < \eta \leq 1$, early stopping gives each coefficient the same shrinkage
factor in expectation, although its uncertainty still depends on feature
sampling and observation noise.

To expose the tradeoff, temporarily fix $\eta_U = 4h$ and $\eta_S = h$. The
common coefficient then has the same mean trajectory under both rules. SD
scaling changes the contraction factor for the mean rare-coefficient error from
$1 - 4hb$ to $1 - h$, while increasing the observation-noise variance added to
that coefficient at each update from $16h^2b\sigma^2$ to $h^2\sigma^2/b$. It
learns the rare signal faster but also introduces more noise.

\section{Oracle risks and the preferred scaling}
\label{sec:conference-predictive}

We now compare the lowest attainable rare-presence risks of the two scaling
rules in the independent model of \cref{sec:conference-setting}. We first give
quantitative gaps when $1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$, corresponding to roughly one
to four expected rare occurrences. We then characterize the oracles for every
fixed positive limiting rescaled budget.

A global learning rate moves both coordinate steps together, while normalization
fixes their ratio. Tuning can change how much each method learns, but cannot
adjust the two steps independently. Lowering the rate reduces observation noise
while slowing learning; stopping sooner also leaves more signal unlearned. The
observation cap limits how far a longer run can compensate for a smaller rate.
The best choice can therefore accept incomplete learning to avoid noise in the
other coefficient.

\paragraph{The oracle comparison.}

Each method chooses its own constant learning rate and iteration count. Let
$\alpha$ denote the rare-coordinate step on the original coefficient scale, so
the update matrices are
\begin{equation}
  D_U(\alpha) = \alpha I_2,\qquad D_S(\alpha)
              = \diag(4b\alpha,\alpha),\quad \alpha
              \geq 0. \label{eq:conference-oracle-rates}
\end{equation}
Here $D = \eta P$, so $\eta_U = \alpha$ and $\eta_S = b\alpha$. Each
parameterization covers every nonnegative constant learning rate for its rule.
At the same $\alpha$, the methods give the rare coefficient the same mean
progress, while SD's common-coordinate step is $4b$ times U's. This complements
the comparison in \cref{sec:conference-setting}, which matched common-coordinate
progress and exposed SD's extra rare-coordinate noise. To learn a dominant rare
signal, U also introduces common-coordinate noise that SD can reduce. To learn a
dominant common signal, SD introduces rare-coordinate noise that U can reduce by
leaving the small rare effect largely unchanged. The oracle chooses $\alpha$
separately for each method; matching progress only explains the constraint each
search faces.

For an observation cap $M \in \mathbb N$, define
\begin{equation}
  R_A^*(M) = \inf_{\substack{\alpha\geq0\\ k\in\{0,\ldots,M\}}} R_{R,k}^{A}(\alpha),\qquad A
           \in \{U,S\}. \label{eq:conference-oracle-risk}
\end{equation}
The oracle chooses a deterministic rate and stop using population risk averaged
over training randomness. It may stop at the prescribed zero initialization.
Because we evaluate finite stops, the search includes rates that need not be
mean-square stable. The cap $M$ applies to each trajectory; the infimum
describes attainable risk, without charging for the search needed to find a rate
and stop. It is not a comparison under equal total tuning computation.

\subsection{Quantitative gaps at finite prevalence}

\begin{theorem}[Opposite oracle rare-risk orderings]
  \label{thm:conference-predictive}
  Suppose $\sigma^2 > 0$, $0 < q \leq 0.04$, and
  $1 \leq \tau = Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$ in the independent binary model above.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $(\beta_R^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_C^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_U^*(M) - R_S^*(M) \geq \sigma^2/1000$.
    \item If $(\beta_C^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_R^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_S^*(M) - R_U^*(M) \geq \sigma^2/50$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Either sign is allowed for each coefficient. Both inequalities also hold if
  each rule searches only its mean-square-stable nonnegative constant rates,
  together with initialization.
\end{theorem}

We prove the theorem in \cref{sec:predictive-proofs} by bounding one method
below at every rate and stop and bounding the other above with a stable
candidate. The diagonal error moments satisfy an exact positive recurrence, even
though the stochastic updates couple the independent features. These bounds
compare the best attainable risks without identifying the minimizing rates and
stops. The stated numerical constants use computer-assisted certification:
exact rational interval calculations certify the analytic bounds over the
continuous parameter regions. \Cref{sec:predictive-certification} describes
the enclosures and coverage checks; the source supplement includes the checker
and its recorded output.

The prevalence-dependent bounds in \cref{sec:predictive-finite-bounds} give
larger gaps in parts of this range. For example, at $q = 0.02$ and $M = 102$,
they give gaps greater than $0.009\sigma^2$ and $0.064\sigma^2$ for the
respective full signal families. \Cref{sec:predictive-certification} gives a
wider prevalence range for the rare-signal family and stronger small-prevalence
constants. These sufficient cutoffs are not transition boundaries.

The expected number of rare occurrences at the cap is $Mq = \tau/(1 - q)$. Hence
the positive gaps persist as the budget grows inversely with prevalence while
rare observations remain scarce. The interval $\tau \in[1,4]$ describes the
budgets covered by the proof, not a general threshold for learning rare
features. Likewise, the two signal families give sufficient conditions for
opposite orderings. The limiting characterization below supplies a transition
along a specified signal-allocation path. Both results concern constant rates,
deterministic stops, final iterates, and population preprocessing; batch
selection, learning-rate schedules, averaging, and data-dependent selection
remain outside their scope.

\paragraph{The compromise after tuning.}

\Cref{fig:tuned-decomposition} separates the risk components at the choices used
in \cref{fig:conference-tuned-intro} for one observation cap. With a dominant
rare signal, U incurs common-coordinate observation noise, while SD keeps that
contribution small and learns more of the rare signal. With a dominant common
signal, SD accepts substantial incomplete learning of that signal together with
rare-coordinate noise. U instead learns the common signal while leaving the
small rare effect largely unchanged. Thus the disadvantaged method need not have
noise as its largest risk component: tuning can favor leaving signal unlearned
to avoid the noise cost of further progress.

\paragraph{Additional common features.}
\label{sec:common-block}

Replacing the common feature with $m$ independent balanced binary features
preserves an exact recurrence for their combined error and the rare error. At
fixed total squared common signal and rare signal, increasing $m$ cannot lower
either method's oracle risk. Numerical searches retain both orderings through
$m = 64$, although their gaps change with dimension.
\Cref{sec:common-block-details} gives the recurrence, proof, and numerical
comparisons; the two-feature separation bounds and limiting characterization are
not asserted for this larger model.

\section{From oracle tuning to validation selection}
\label{sec:conference-experiments}

The oracle fixes its rate and stop before observing training. Validation instead
selects among fitted predictors. We examine this distinction under controlled
changes to the theorem's setup and assess how missing rare validation
observations affect selection.

\subsection{Controlled departures from the oracle model}
\label{sec:theory-bridge}

We retain the independent binary model, coefficient pairs $(0.1,1)$ and
$(1,0.1)$, and noise variance one. At $q = .02$ and $.005$, caps give roughly
one, two, or four expected rare occurrences. Eight training arms separately
introduce observation reuse, estimated preprocessing, and intercept fitting,
then combine them. Reuse draws from 256 noisy training rows. Empirical
preprocessing uses all 256 rows, so its data requirements can exceed the SGD
observation cap. \Cref{sec:theory-bridge-protocol} gives the full protocol.

For each scaling and training arm, we calibrate a rate factor and stop to
minimize mean rare risk, then evaluate those fixed choices on independent
training runs. With estimated preprocessing, the factor determines a constant
rate from the training data. Both orderings persist in all 96 comparisons: SD
has lower rare risk for the dominant rare signal, and U has lower rare risk for
the dominant common signal (\cref{fig:bridge-controlled}). This evidence
concerns the tested signal pairs and a conservative finite rate search; it does
not extend the theorem to these training procedures.

\paragraph{Missing rare validation observations.}

Missing rare validation observations create a separate obstacle. For the fresh
baseline at $q = .005$, $M = 804$, and the dominant rare signal, selection with
128 validation rows changes the SD-minus-U gap from $-.0216$ to $+1.133$ (MCSE
$.062$). The rare group is absent in 2206 of 4096 validation sets, triggering
the declared fallback to aggregate loss. Conditional on absence, the gap is
$+2.193$; when the group is present, it is $-.105$. Even this conservative
search can miss the group on which the methods differ most
(\cref{sec:theory-bridge-protocol,fig:bridge-validation}).

\section{Discussion}

Our limiting characterization identifies the lowest rare-subgroup risks
attainable by each scaling after optimizing its constant learning rate and
deterministic stopping time. Uniform control over the full rate-and-stop domain
reduces these oracles to scalar minimizations for arbitrary fixed signals and
noise. With positive noise, the formulas prove a unique crossing along
allocations of fixed positive initial limiting subgroup risk. The separate
finite-prevalence theorem supplies explicit positive gaps on two signal
families while rare observations remain scarce.

The characterization explains the noise cost of learning each signal. Unscaled
training can learn the common signal with vanishing noise cost while leaving the rare
effect unchanged; standardization can instead avoid the common-coordinate noise
cost of learning a rare effect. Noise and budget determine which compromise is
preferable. Normalization therefore determines how a finite-budget learner
responds to the evidence for each feature, even after rate and stopping choices
have been optimized.

Both theorems concern two independent binary features, known population
preprocessing, zero initialization, a fixed zero intercept, and final iterates
under fresh sampling. Finite-prevalence searches show that convergence to the
limiting decision can be slow enough to change the preferred scaling. The
large-budget ratio $4/5$ describes a further limit of the limiting functions,
not a simultaneous limit in prevalence and budget. Learning-rate schedules,
averaging, batch selection, data-dependent stopping, and adaptive optimizers
remain outside the comparison.

Replacing the common feature with an independent block preserves the exact
moment reduction. At fixed total signal strength, increasing the number of
common features cannot lower either method's oracle risk. The numerical
comparison retains both orderings through 64 common features, with dimension
changing the size of the gaps. Uniform separation bounds for this larger model
remain open.

The controlled study shows that both orderings can survive changes to the
training procedure. They persist for the tested signal pairs under observation
reuse, estimated preprocessing, and intercept fitting when tuning choices are
fixed before evaluation. Selecting a predictor after observing training can
reverse an ordering, even with exact population risk. An extension to adaptive
selection would therefore need to account for the benefit each scaling gains
from choosing among its realized predictors.

Feature normalization therefore belongs in the statistical specification of
training under a finite budget: even in a two-feature model, tuning a global
learning rate and stopping time need not make different relative scalings
equivalent in rare-subgroup risk.

\section{Proof of the comparison after oracle tuning}
\label{sec:predictive-proofs}

We prove \cref{thm:conference-predictive} directly at positive prevalence. The
bounds below are explicit functions of $q$ and $\tau = Mq(1 - q)$. Finite minima
over rate intervals bound the continuous search; they do not restrict the oracle
to a rate grid. Analytic bounds cover every rate above one. The checker
\texttt{checks/predictive\_fixed\_q.py} certifies the reported positive regions
with rational arithmetic and checks the moment bounds against observation-level
tensor calculations.

The source supplement also includes small-prevalence checks in
\path{checks/predictive_theorem.py} and \path{checks/predictive_review.py}.

\subsection{Exact moments}

By homogeneity, take $\sigma^2 = 1$. Put $s = (\beta_C^*)^2$,
$r = (\beta_R^*)^2$, $b = q(1 - q)$, and $w = (1 - q)^2$. Write
$C_k = \E\bigl((\beta_{C,k} - \beta_C^*)^2\bigr)$ and
$H_k = \E\bigl((\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2\bigr)$. Independence gives
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k} = C_k/8 + wH_k/2. \label{eq:predictive-risk}
\end{equation}
The required feature moments are $x_C^2 = 1/4$, $\E(x_R^2) = b$,
$\E(x_R^4) = b(1 - 3b)$, and $\E(x_C^2x_R^2) = b/4$. Mixed moments with an odd
power of $x_C$ vanish. Expanding the squared update and using the conditional
noise assumptions gives
\begin{equation}
  \binom{C_{k+1}}{H_{k+1}} = \begin{pmatrix}
                               A           & 4u^2b \\
                               b\alpha^2/4 & D_b
                             \end{pmatrix}\binom{C_k}{H_k}
                             + \binom{4u^2}{b\alpha^2},\qquad \binom{C_0}{H_0}
                           = \binom{s}{r}, \label{eq:predictive-moments}
\end{equation}
where $A = (1 - u)^2$, $D_b = 1 - b\alpha(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha)$, and
$u = \alpha/4$ for U or $u = b\alpha$ for S. The diagonal equations and risk do
not involve cross error moments, so coefficient signs do not affect risk. Every
matrix entry is nonnegative for every $\alpha \geq 0$: in particular,
$D_b = (1 - b\alpha)^2 + b\alpha^2(1 - 4b) \geq 0$. Increasing either initial
squared coefficient cannot reduce risk.

For $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, the row sums are
\[
  1 - u\{2 - u(1 + 4b)\} < 1,\qquad 1 - b\alpha\{2 - 5\alpha/4 + 3b\alpha\} < 1.
\]
The homogeneous moments contract and the noise moments stay bounded. The cross
moment is bounded by $\sqrt{C_kH_k}$, so all positive candidate rates used below
are mean-square stable. At zero rate, the predictor stays at initialization.

\subsection{Explicit finite-prevalence bounds}
\label{sec:predictive-finite-bounds}

Throughout, $0 < q \leq 1/16$ and $1 \leq \tau \leq 4$. Let $N = 4096$ and
$a_j = j/N$, for $j = 0,\ldots,N$. Define
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    d_b(a) & = a(2 - a + 3ba),                                                            \\
    n_j    & = \min\left\{ w,\frac{wa_j}{2 - a_j + 3ba_j} + \frac{a_j}{8 - a_j} \right\}, \\
    p_j    & = \min\left\{ \frac18,\frac{wa_j}{2(2 - ba_j)} \right\},
  \end{aligned}
\]
and
\[
  z_{R,j} = \exp\left\{ -\frac{\tau d_b(a_{j+1})}
                                  {1 - bd_b(a_{j+1})} \right\},\qquad z_{C,j}
          = \exp\left\{ -\frac{2\tau a_{j+1}}{1 - ba_{j+1}} \right\}.
\]
Define the lower bounds
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    L_R(q,\tau) & = \min\left\{ \frac{w}{2(1 + 3b)}, \min_{0 \leq j<N}\frac{n_j + (w - n_j)z_{R,j}}2 \right\}, \\
    L_C(q,\tau) & = \min_{0 \leq j<N}\left\{ p_j + \left( \frac18 - p_j \right)z_{C,j} \right\}.
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-finite-lower}
\end{equation}
The integer $N$ controls only the precision of the lower bounds. All their
denominators are positive on the stated domain.

For the upper bounds, use $k = M$ and the stable rates
\[
  \alpha_S = \frac{2}{\tau + 4},\qquad \alpha_U = \frac{8b}{\tau} = \frac8M.
\]
Put
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    p_S = \frac{\alpha_S(1 - q)}{2 - (1 - q)\alpha_S},\qquad G = \frac{\alpha_U}{2 - \alpha_U/4}, \\
    H_* = \frac{1/100 + b\alpha_U/(2 - \alpha_U/4) + \tau\alpha_U^2(1 + G/4)}
               {1 - \tau b\alpha_U^2G/4}.
  \end{gathered}
\]
Here $\alpha_S \leq 2/5$, $\alpha_U \leq 1/2$, and
$\tau b\alpha_U^2G/4 \leq 1/960$, so $H_*$ is well defined. Define
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    V_S(q,\tau) & = \frac12\left[ p_S + \left( w + \frac1{400} - p_S \right) e^{-\tau\alpha_S\{2-(1-q)\alpha_S\}} \right], \\
    V_U(q,\tau) & = \frac{e^{-4}}8 + \frac G8 + \left( \frac w2 + \frac{bG}8 \right)H_*.
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-finite-upper}
\end{equation}
Define the gap bounds
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_R(q,\tau) = L_R(q,\tau) - V_S(q,\tau),\qquad \Delta_C(q,\tau)
                   = L_C(q,\tau)
                     - V_U(q,\tau). \label{eq:predictive-finite-gaps}
\end{equation}
For the rare-signal and common-signal families, respectively, we will prove
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    R_U^*(M) - R_S^*(M) & \geq \Delta_R(q,\tau), \\
    R_S^*(M) - R_U^*(M) & \geq \Delta_C(q,\tau),
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-oracle-gaps}
\end{equation}
throughout $0 < q \leq 1/16$ and $1 \leq \tau \leq 4$, with $\sigma^2 = 1$ as
above. For general positive noise variance, multiply both bounds by $\sigma^2$.
The bounds need not be positive everywhere on this domain. A nonpositive bound
does not establish the opposite ordering.

\subsection{Dominant rare signal}

Suppose $r = 1$ and $s \leq 1/100$. For U and $\alpha \in[0,1]$,
\[
  D_b - A = \frac{(1 - 4b)\alpha}{16} \{8 + (12b - 1)\alpha\} \geq 0.
\]
Dropping nonnegative coupling terms in \cref{eq:predictive-moments} and summing
the direct noise terms gives, with $z = D_b^k$,
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k}^U \geq \frac12\left[ wz + \left\{ \frac{w\alpha}{2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{8 - \alpha} \right\}(1 - z) \right]. \label{eq:predictive-u-lower}
\end{equation}
For the common-noise term, use $1 - A^k \geq 1 - D_b^k$. The formula extends
directly to zero rate. Its right side is affine in $z$. Taking the smaller value
at $z = 1$ or $z = D_b^M$ is equivalent to capping the noise coefficient at $w$.

For $0 \leq x < 1$, the logarithm series gives $-\log(1 - x) \leq x/(1 - x)$.
Consequently,
\[
  D_b^M \geq \exp\left\{ -\frac{\tau d_b(\alpha)}
                                      {1 - bd_b(\alpha)} \right\}.
\]
Both $d_b(\alpha)$ and the noise coefficient increase on $[0,1]$. For
$\alpha \in[a_j,a_{j+1}]$, use the left endpoint for the capped noise
coefficient and the right endpoint for the exponential lower bound. This gives
the $j$th term in $L_R$ for every $0 \leq k \leq M$.

For every $\alpha \geq 1$, put $m_b = 1/(1 + 3b)$. The identity
\[
  D_bm_b + b\alpha^2 - m_b = \frac{2b\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{1 + 3b} \geq 0
\]
and positivity of the recurrence imply $H_k \geq m_b$ by induction from
$H_0 = 1$. Thus $R_{R,k}^U \geq w/\{2(1 + 3b)\}$ at every finite stop, including
zero. This proves the remaining term in $L_R$, without a stability restriction.

For the S candidate, retain the exact risk weight by setting
$W_k = C_k/4 + wH_k = 2R_{R,k}^S$. Let
$\rho = 1 - b\alpha\{2 - (1 - q)\alpha\}$. The recurrence gives
\[
  W_{k+1} \leq \rho W_k + b\alpha^2(1 - q),
\]
because the common coefficient has multiplier $\rho$ and
\[
  \rho w - (wD_b + b^3\alpha^2) = 2wbq(1 - 2q)\alpha^2 \geq 0.
\]
At $\alpha = \alpha_S$, $0 < \rho < 1$ and $W_0 \leq w + 1/400$. Geometric
summation therefore bounds the risk by
\[
  \frac12\left[ p_S + (w + 1/400 - p_S)\rho^M \right] \leq V_S(q,\tau).
\]
The last step uses $\rho^M \leq e^{-\tau\alpha_S\{2-(1-q)\alpha_S\}}$ and
$w + 1/400 > p_S$. Hence every U rate and stop exceeds this S candidate's risk
by at least $\Delta_R(q,\tau)$, uniformly over the rare-signal family.

\subsection{Dominant common signal}

Suppose $s = 1$ and $r \leq 1/100$. Under S, put $A = (1 - b\alpha)^2$. Since
$D_b - A = b\alpha^2(1 - 4b) \geq 0$, positivity gives
\[
  C_k \geq A^k,\qquad H_k
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}D_b^j
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A^j.
\]
For $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, geometric summation yields
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \frac{A^k}{8} + \frac{w\alpha}{2(2 - b\alpha)}(1 - A^k).
\]
The expression is affine in $A^k$. Minimizing over $k \leq M$ caps its second
coefficient at $1/8$ and replaces $A^k$ by $A^M$. Moreover,
\[
  A^M \geq \exp\left\{ -\frac{2\tau\alpha}{1 - b\alpha} \right\}.
\]
The plateau and the magnitude of the exponent both increase with $\alpha$. Their
left and right endpoints give the $j$th term in $L_C$ on each rate interval.
Zero rate gives risk at least $1/8$ directly.

For $\alpha \geq 1$, if $A \geq 1$ then $C_k \geq 1$ and the risk is at least
$1/8$. Otherwise $0 < b\alpha < 2$ and the same geometric bound gives
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \min\left\{ \frac18, \frac{w\alpha}{2(2 - b\alpha)} \right\}
            \geq \min\left\{ \frac18,\frac{w}{2(2 - b)} \right\}
            = \frac18.
\]
The last equality follows from $q \leq 1/16$. Since $L_C \leq 1/8$, the bound
covers all larger rates as well.

For the U candidate, set $\alpha = \alpha_U$, $u = \alpha/4$, and
$H_{\max} = \max_{0 \leq j \leq M}H_j$. Summing the common equation gives
\[
  C_j \leq A^j + G(1 + bH_{\max}).
\]
Since $D_b \leq 1$, drop the rare-coordinate contraction and sum up to any
$j \leq M$. Using $\sum_{j \geq 0}A^j = 1/\{u(2 - u)\}$ gives
\[
  H_{\max} \leq \frac1{100}
                + \frac{b\alpha}{2 - \alpha/4}
                + \tau\alpha^2\left( 1 + \frac G4 \right)
                + \frac{\tau b\alpha^2G}{4}H_{\max}.
\]
Thus $H_{\max} \leq H_*$. Also,
$A^M = (1 - \alpha/4)^{2M} \leq e^{-\alpha M/2} = e^{-4}$, which gives
$R_{R,M}^U \leq V_U(q,\tau)$. Every S rate and stop therefore exceeds this U
candidate's risk by at least $\Delta_C(q,\tau)$, uniformly over the
common-signal family.

Taking the two oracle infima proves \cref{eq:predictive-oracle-gaps}.
Restricting each search to stable rates preserves both lower bounds and both
candidates. Rescaling by $\sigma^2$ gives the bounds for general positive noise
variance. The certificates below then imply \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.
The argument requires neither stability of the losing method's rate nor
attainment of either infimum.

  P_{18}(x)
  \leq e^x
  \leq P_{18}(x)
       + \frac{x^{19}/19!}{1 - x/20}. \label{eq:predictive-exp-certificate}

over continuous prevalence and budget ranges, including every admissible integer
stop. No sampled rate or prevalence search is used as proof.

  Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:conference-predictive}, if

The last two rows of the table prove the corollary. The certified gaps grow as

\section{One rare feature and several common features}
\label{sec:common-block-details}

\subsection{Moment reduction}

Let $I_1,\ldots,I_m$ be independent Bernoulli variables with prevalence $1/2$,
and let $J$ be an independent Bernoulli variable with prevalence $q$, where
$m \geq 1$ and $0 < q < 1/2$. With population-centered features, the response is
\[
  y = \sum_{j=1}^m\beta_j^*(I_j - 1/2)
      + \beta_R^*(J - q)
      + \varepsilon, \qquad \E(\varepsilon \mid x)
    = 0, \qquad \E(\varepsilon^2 \mid x)
    = \sigma^2.
\]
Training uses fresh observations, zero initialization, and a fixed zero
intercept. On the original coefficient scale, the rare-coordinate step is
$\alpha$ and every common-coordinate step is $4u$, with $u = \alpha/4$ for U or
$u = b\alpha$ for S. Here $b = q(1 - q)$, as before. Define
$s = \sum_{j=1}^m(\beta_j^*)^2$, $r = (\beta_R^*)^2$, and
\[
  V_{j,k} = \E[(\beta_{j,k} - \beta_j^*)^2],\qquad G_k
          = \frac14\sum_{j=1}^m V_{j,k},\qquad H_k
          = \E[(\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2].
\]

The combined moments satisfy
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    G_{k+1} & = (1 - 2u + mu^2)G_k + mu^2(bH_k + \sigma^2), \\
    H_{k+1} & = D_bH_k + b\alpha^2(G_k + \sigma^2),
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:common-block-moments}
\end{equation}
with $(G_0,H_0) = (s/4,r)$ and $D_b = 1 - b\alpha(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha)$. The
factors of $m$ show how additional common features increase the noise cost, even
when their true coefficients are zero.

Expanding the squared SGD update for a common coefficient gives
\[
  V_{j,k+1} = (1 - u)^2V_{j,k}
              + u^2\sum_{\substack{\ell=1\\\ell\ne j}}^m V_{\ell,k}
              + 4u^2bH_k
              + 4u^2\sigma^2.
\]
The common feature has second moment $1/4$ and fourth moment $1/16$.
Independence and centering remove terms involving two distinct coefficient
errors from this diagonal equation, even when those errors are correlated.
Summing over $j$ and dividing by four gives the first line of
\cref{eq:common-block-moments}. The rare equation follows from
$\E[(J - q)^4] = b(1 - 3b)$ and the same expansion. Conditioning the test
distribution on $J = 1$ leaves the common features centered and independent, so
the conditional second-moment matrix is diagonal and
$R_{R,k} = \{G_k + (1 - q)^2H_k\}/2$.

The recurrence depends on the common coefficients only through $s$. Thus the
expected rare risk is unchanged if the same squared signal is concentrated in
one common feature or spread across the block. Coefficient signs also have no
effect on this risk. Setting $m = 1$ recovers \cref{eq:predictive-moments}, with
$G_k = C_k/4$ and with the observation-noise terms multiplied by $\sigma^2$.

\subsection{Comparison across dimensions}

For fixed $q,s,r,\sigma^2$, write $R_A^*(M;m)$ for the infimum of rare risk
under scaling $A$ over every nonnegative constant rate and deterministic stop
$k \in \{0,\ldots,M\}$ in the model with $m$ common features.

\begin{proposition}[Increasing the common-feature dimension]
  \label{prop:common-block-dimension}
  For either scaling $A \in \{U,S\}$ and integers $m_2 \geq m_1 \geq 1$,
  \[
    R_A^*(M;m_2) \geq R_A^*(M;m_1).
  \]
  The same inequality holds at each fixed nonnegative rare-coordinate step
  $\alpha$ and stop $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  At fixed $\alpha$, the value of $u$ does not depend on $m$. Every coefficient
  in \cref{eq:common-block-moments} is nonnegative. In particular,
  \[
    1 - 2u + mu^2 = (1 - u)^2 + (m - 1)u^2
                  \geq 0, \qquad D_b
                  = (1 - b\alpha)^2 + b\alpha^2(1 - 4b)
                  \geq 0.
  \]
  Increasing $m$ increases the coefficients and noise term in the common
  equation and leaves the rare equation unchanged. Both systems start at
  $(s/4,r)$, so induction gives coordinatewise larger moments at every stop. The
  risk weights are nonnegative and independent of $m$, proving the fixed-rate
  comparison. Taking the infimum over the same rates and stops proves the oracle
  comparison.
\end{proof}

The proposition applies separately to each method. It does not imply that their
risk difference is monotone in $m$, or that either ordering from the two-feature
theorem persists.

\subsection{Numerical comparisons}
\label{sec:common-block-numerics}

Both orderings persist in all 126 numerical comparisons over seven dimensions,
three prevalences, three caps, and the two signal allocations. Each scaling
searches its own finite grid of stable rates and every integer stop within the
cap. \Cref{fig:common-block} shows the slice with roughly two expected rare
observations. When the rare signal dominates and $q = 10^{-4}$, increasing $m$
from one to 64 raises U's selected risk from $0.256$ to $0.500$, while SD's
changes from $0.229$ to $0.230$. When the common signal dominates, U retains its
advantage, though that advantage narrows with dimension, especially at the
larger prevalence. These comparisons extend the numerical evidence to more
features; they do not extend the uniform gaps in
\cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

We use $m \in \{1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}$, $q \in \{0.02,0.005,10^{-4}\}$,
$\sigma^2 = 1$, and $(s,r) \in \{(0.01,1),\allowbreak(1,0.01)\}$. The
observation caps are

  \begin{array}{c|rrr}
       q    &     \multicolumn{3}{c}{M}      \\

  \end{array}

These approximate $Mq(1 - q) = 1,2,4$ while remaining in $[1,4]$. The initial
values $(G_0,H_0) = (s/4,r)$ are the same across dimensions. The study uses
exact expected moments evaluated in floating point; it does not simulate
training or estimate risks from a test sample.

Each scaling uses zero rate and 1,025 positive rates geometrically spaced
between $10^{-7}$ and $0.999$ times its mean-square stability limit. To compute
that limit, write $u = c\alpha$, where $c = 1/4$ for U or $c = b$ for S, and let
$T$ be the homogeneous transition in \cref{eq:common-block-moments}. Then
\[
  \det(I - T) = bc\alpha^2(4 - L\alpha + K\alpha^2),\qquad L
              = 2(1 - 3b + mc),\qquad K
              = mc(1 - 4b).
\]
The first positive root gives
\[
  \alpha_{\mathrm{stab}} = \frac{8}{L + \sqrt{L^2 - 16K}}.
\]
The search includes every integer stop from zero through each cap. It selects
the rate and stop that minimize expected rare risk, before any training
realization is observed. This finite stable-rate search is narrower than the
unrestricted oracle in \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

Both signal orderings persist in all 126 paired comparisons. The smallest
selected gaps are $0.01959$ in favor of SD for the dominant rare signal and
$0.00633$ in favor of U for the dominant common signal. A nested coarse search
retains alternate positive rates and zero, giving 513 positive rates. Refining
this grid changes individual selected risks by at most $3.50 \times 10^{-5}$ and
preserves every ordering. No positive-stop selection uses either endpoint of the
positive rate grid. These checks measure sensitivity to the tested grid; they do
not certify its distance from the continuous optimum.

\subsection{Oracle risk and its components}

\Cref{fig:conference-tuned-intro} illustrates the two signal families in
\cref{thm:conference-predictive} at $q = 10^{-4}$ and $\sigma^2 = 1$. We set
$(\beta_C^*,\beta_R^*) = (0.1,1)$ for the rare-signal panel and $(1,0.1)$ for
the common-signal panel. All other assumptions match the theorem. We evaluate 61
integer observation caps, evenly spaced up to rounding from $10{,}002$ to
$40{,}004$, so $1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$. The horizontal axis reports the
expected number of rare observations at the cap, $Mq$, rather than a realized
count.

For each rule, we use the parameterization in \cref{eq:conference-oracle-rates}
and a grid consisting of zero and 4097 geometrically spaced positive values of
$\alpha$ from $10^{-6}$ to $2$. We iterate the exact recurrence
\labelcref{eq:predictive-moments}, including both sources of training variance.
At each cap, we select the rate and integer stopping count with the lowest
population rare risk, searching every stop from zero through the cap. The curves
connect these numerical minima; they are not trajectories of a single trained
model. Selection uses population risk averaged over training randomness, rather
than validation observations.

We repeat the search with every second positive grid point and check all 244
selected endpoints against a separate full tensor moment operator constructed by
enumerating the four binary observations. The reproduction script requires the
maximum risk change on grid refinement to be below $2 \times 10^{-6}$ and the
maximum tensor replay discrepancy to be below $10^{-10}$. All selected rates are
interior to the grid. These checks assess numerical accuracy within the stated
search; the theorem supplies the separate comparison over all nonnegative
constant rates. The figure illustrates an existing theoretical result and is not
a prospective evaluation of its predictions.

\Cref{fig:tuned-decomposition} uses the same selections at cap $M = 20{,}503$.
We compute the squared mean error, noiseless feature-sampling covariance, and
observation-noise covariance with the full moment recurrence, then weight each
coordinate by its contribution to rare risk. The saved summary retains these
components for every selected endpoint. We check each component against the
separately enumerated tensor operator and require its discrepancy, and that
between the component sum and the selected risk, to be below $10^{-10}$.

\subsubsection{Variance decomposition}
\label{sec:variance-decomposition}

Write $D = \eta P$ and $C = \E(xx^\top)$. To prove
\cref{eq:conference-decomposition}, we run the noiseless recursion on the same
feature stream. Its mean error is $e_k = -(I - DC)^k\beta^*$ and its covariance
is $A_k \succeq 0$. Conditional on all sampled features, the component due to
noise has mean zero, so its cross moment with the noiseless component vanishes.
Its covariance is $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$, which proves the decomposition.
Equivalently, if
$\mathcal T_D(V) = \E\bigl((I - Dxx^\top)V(I - xx^\top D)\bigr)$, then
\[
  V_k = \mathcal T_D^k(\beta^*\beta^{*\top})
        + \sigma^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal T_D^j(DCD).
\]
The first term is the noiseless second moment $e_ke_k^\top + A_k$, and the
second is the observation-noise covariance $\sigma^2N_k$.

\subsection{Paths to a shared least-squares solution}
\label{sec:coefficient-paths}

\Cref{fig:intro-coefficient-paths} uses a constructed fixed dataset of 200
observations. The binary cells
$(I,J) = (0,0),\allowbreak(0,1),\allowbreak(1,0),\allowbreak(1,1)$ have counts
$98,2,98,2$, respectively. We center the columns by $(1/2,0.02)$, set
$y = x_C + x_R$ without noise, and fix the centered intercept at zero. Thus
$X^\top X/200 = \diag(1/4,0.0196)$ and the unique OLS solution is $(1,1)$. Both
methods start at zero and use the full gradient of empirical half squared loss.
The unscaled learning rate is $0.4$, and the standardized learning rate is

\section{Empirical methods}
\label{sec:empirical-methods}

The controlled study isolates changes to the oracle training model and compares
calibration with adaptive selection. All comparisons pair the scaling rules
within each replication. The protocols below specify which data and candidates
each comparison uses.

\subsection{Controlled departures from the oracle model}
\label{sec:theory-bridge-protocol}

The study in \cref{sec:theory-bridge} uses
$I \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)$ and $J \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(q)$
independently, with $q \in \{.02,.005\}$. Responses are
$Y = \beta_C^*(I - 1/2) + \beta_R^*(J - q) + \varepsilon$, where
$\varepsilon \sim N(0,1)$ is independent and the coefficient pairs are $(.1,1)$
and $(1,.1)$. Each update processes one observation. Caps are $(52,102,204)$ at
$q = .02$ and $(202,402,804)$ at $q = .005$, all satisfying
$1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$. Settings were specified before examining the study's
outcomes; this is an exploratory extension of the existing theorem.

\subsubsection{Training arms and pairing}

\Cref{tab:bridge-arms} specifies the eight arms. The first 256 rows of an
independent feature and noise stream form the fixed training pool. Fresh SGD
uses successive rows of that stream; reuse samples pool indices independently
with replacement, retaining each row's original noise. Common random numbers
pair the two scalings, signal pairs, training arms, and candidate paths within
each replication. Estimated preprocessing uses the entire pool, including rows
beyond the SGD cap when $M < 256$. These arms therefore use additional data for
preprocessing, beyond the observations counted by the update cap.

All feature coefficients start at zero. Empirical variances use denominator 256,
and a zero variance receives divisor one. No additional coefficient freezing is
applied. Thus empirical centering automatically makes a constant training
column's gradient zero, while population centering can leave it nonzero. Only
arm 8 initializes the fitted intercept at the training response mean. There are
21 training pools without rare observations at $q = .02$ and 1148 at $q = .005$,
out of 4096 evaluation replications each.

\subsubsection{Candidate family and calibration}

Let $z(i,j)$ denote the transformed feature vector under a given arm and
scaling, augmented by a unit coordinate when fitting an intercept. Define
\[
  L = \max_{(i,j) \in \{0,1\}^2}\|z(i,j)\|^2, \qquad \eta = t/L.
\]
The rate factors are zero and 129 geometric values from $10^{-4}$ to $1.9$. Each
homogeneous update $I - \eta zz^\top$ is nonexpansive because
$0 \leq \eta\|z\|^2 \leq 1.9 < 2$. This condition rules out expansion by an
individual homogeneous update; it does not bound accumulated response noise or
guarantee good predictions on an unseen subgroup. It is a conservative search
restriction, not a characterization of every stable constant rate. For estimated
preprocessing, $L$ depends on the training pool. Calibration fixes $t$, while
each resulting trajectory retains one constant $\eta$.

Stopping checkpoints are the distinct integers obtained by rounding 65 geometric
points from one to the largest cap, together with zero and all three caps. A
nested coarse grid retains every second positive rate factor and every second
checkpoint, always including zero and the caps. Both scalings receive the same
number of trajectories. At cap $M$, nominal search cost is $130M$ processed
observations per arm, scaling, signal, and replication; caps and selection
policies share paths. Preprocessing, validation scoring, and calibration require
additional resources.

For each prevalence, 2048 calibration replications determine the mean exact
population rare risk of every candidate. Each arm, scaling, signal, and cap then
selects a rate factor and eligible stop. The fresh, fixed-intercept arm instead
uses exact expected moments for this choice. An independent set of 4096
evaluation replications assesses the frozen choices. The two stages use distinct
random streams derived from seed 20260924. This procedure estimates a
finite-family optimum conditional on the calibration run, rather than the
unrestricted oracle in \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

\subsubsection{Effects of changing training}

Both orderings persist in all 96 comparisons: SD has lower rare risk for the
dominant rare signal, and U has lower rare risk for the dominant common signal
(\cref{fig:bridge-controlled}). Each gap exceeds nine paired Monte Carlo
standard errors in magnitude, conditional on the calibration choices. A coarser
rate and stopping grid preserves all 96 signs. These results concern the two
tested signal pairs and do not extend the theorem to the changed training
procedures.

The size of the gap nevertheless depends on how training changes. For the
dominant rare signal at $q = .005$ and $M = 804$, the calibrated SD-minus-U gap
is $-.0216$ under fresh training. Reuse changes the gap by $+.00943$, and
estimating scales adds $+.00710$, with paired standard errors $.00093$ and
$.00045$. Both changes narrow SD's advantage. Fitting an intercept with
population preprocessing instead changes the reuse gap by $-.0440$ (standard
error $.00345$), widening that advantage. Thus the preferred scaling can stay
the same while the cost of choosing the other scaling changes substantially.

\subsubsection{Adaptive selection and exact evaluation}

Each evaluation replication also supplies an independent validation dataset with
2048 rows. Nested prefixes of sizes 128, 512, and 2048 select candidates by rare
validation half squared loss, using aggregate loss if the rare group is empty.
Ties favor an earlier stop, then a smaller rate factor. Empty rare validation
groups occur in $(306,1,0)$ replications at $q = .02$ and $(2206,326,0)$ at
$q = .005$, in increasing sample-size order. The validation selectors never
observe population risk.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[]{../figures/theory-bridge/validation-gaps.pdf}
  \caption{
    Changing selection on the same candidate paths at the largest cap for each
    prevalence. Calibrated choices are fixed before evaluation; the other points
    select by rare validation loss with the indicated sample size. Negative gaps

  }

\end{figure}

A further benchmark selects each replication's realized predictor with lowest
exact population rare risk. To distinguish the two objectives, let
$r_R(\widehat f_{D,a})$ be the population rare excess risk of candidate $a$
trained on random data and sampling choices $D$. For a fixed finite candidate
index set $\mathcal A$, including the allowed rate factors and stops,
\[
  \E_D\!\left[ \min_{a \in \mathcal A}r_R(\widehat f_{D,a}) \right] \leq \min_{a \in \mathcal A}\E_D\!\left[ r_R(\widehat f_{D,a}) \right].
\]

The left side allows adaptation to realized training randomness. The calibrated
procedure approximates the right side, with its choices learned on separate
replications. Neither side here is an unrestricted rate search, and a lower left
side for each scaling does not preserve their ordering. The reversal under exact
selection in \cref{tab:bridge-selection} therefore concerns a different
objective from the theorem, even without validation noise.

\Cref{fig:bridge-validation} shows how validation selection changes the
comparisons across both prevalences and signals. Missing validation observations
create a further problem. For the fresh baseline with $q = .005$, $M = 804$, and
a dominant rare signal, validation with 128 rows changes the gap from $-.0216$
to $+1.133$ (standard error $.062$). The rare group is absent in 2206 of 4096
validation sets. Conditional on its absence, the gap is $+2.193$; when it is
present, the gap is $-.105$. Even within this conservative rate search,
validation can miss the group on which the two scalings differ most.

Evaluation enumerates the four binary cells analytically and excludes
irreducible test noise. For a predictor written in population-centered
coordinates as $a + b_C(I - 1/2) + b_R(J - q)$, its rare excess risk is
\[
  r_R = \frac{(b_C - \beta_C^*)^2}{8}
        + \frac{\{a + (1 - q)(b_R - \beta_R^*)\}^2}{2}.
\]
This includes the fitted intercept and any shift induced by estimated centering.
No prediction clipping is applied. We average paired SD-minus-U differences
across evaluation replications and report their Monte Carlo standard errors.
These errors condition on the frozen calibration choices; they exclude
calibration uncertainty and adjustments for multiple comparisons.

\subsection{Numerical checks}

Independent implementations check transformed-coordinate SGD against
original-coordinate updates, the analytical losses against four-cell
enumeration, and baseline moments against a full tensor recurrence. Replaying
one evaluation replication from every shard checks 73,728 selected endpoints,
with maximum coefficient discrepancy below $1.7 \times 10^{-14}$. All saved
selected predictors pass the cell-enumeration risk check. No calibration or
evaluation candidate fails numerically, and no fine calibrated choice lies at a
positive rate boundary.

For the fresh fixed-intercept baseline, a denser exact search uses 1025 positive
rate factors and every integer stop. The largest excess of the chosen fine-grid
risk over this denser minimum is $.000109$ at $q = .02$ and $.000204$ at
$q = .005$. Evaluation means differ from their exact expectations by at most
$1.98$ Monte Carlo standard errors. These checks assess the baseline grids and
simulation, not continuous optimality for all arms.

The protocol and implementation are in \path{experiments/theory_bridge/};
\path{results/theory-bridge/} retains settings, raw feature cells and noise,
replacement indices, calibration summaries, selected predictors and their
indices, numerical failure records, and the machine-readable summary. The
summary includes aggregate and rare risks, matched changes between arms,
coarse-grid comparisons, selection regret, and coverage diagnostics. The checks
are in \path{checks/theory_bridge.py}. Summarization and plotting use saved
results without training.
