\title{Feature Normalization under Finite Training Budgets}
\altauthor{\Name{Anonymous Authors}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  Standardizing a rare feature can help stochastic gradient descent learn its
  effect within a finite budget, but it also changes the noise cost of learning.
  We show that tuning the learning rate and stopping time need not remove the
  resulting differences in rare-subgroup risk. For two independent binary
  features, standardization attains lower risk on a family with a dominant rare
  signal, whereas unscaled training attains lower risk on a family with a
  dominant common signal. Each method may choose any nonnegative constant rate
  and any deterministic stop within the same observation cap. With known
  population preprocessing and one fresh observation per update, the gaps remain
  bounded away from zero as prevalence decreases while the expected number of
  rare occurrences stays roughly between one and four. Exact moments explain the
  competing costs of incomplete learning and training variation, and numerical
  comparisons explore intermediate signals and additional common features. In a
  controlled study, both orderings persist under observation reuse, estimated
  preprocessing, and intercept fitting when tuning choices are fixed before
  evaluation. Selecting a predictor after observing training can reverse an
  ordering, even when selection uses exact population risk.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
  Feature normalization, stochastic gradient descent, early stopping,
  rare-subgroup risk
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}

A rare feature gives a model few opportunities to learn its effect. Without
scaling, gradient descent may fit common features while leaving the rare feature
largely unlearned. Dividing by the feature's standard deviation increases the
influence of those few observations. This can help recover a useful rare signal,
but it also makes the fitted coefficient more sensitive to noise.

These effects can substantially change prediction where the rare feature is
present, despite contributing little to average error. Normalization therefore
raises a statistical question: how strongly should training respond to scarce
evidence? Scaling leaves the full-rank least-squares solution unchanged but
changes which signals we learn before stopping.
\Cref{fig:intro-coefficient-paths} illustrates this difference on a fixed
least-squares problem: the methods approach the same solution along different
paths and reach different coefficients when stopped early.

Adjusting the learning rate and stopping time is a natural way to compensate for
different learning speeds. Our main result shows that this adjustment need not
erase the risk difference. \Cref{fig:intro-signal-crossing} illustrates the
central comparison: standardization gives lower rare-subgroup risk when the rare
signal dominates, whereas unscaled training gives lower risk when the common
signal dominates. Each rule selects its own rate and stop under the same
observation cap. The curves trace the transition as signal moves from the common
feature to the rare feature at a fixed budget and noise level.
\Cref{fig:conference-tuned-intro} shows the two signal families across
observation caps.

Our main contribution is an oracle comparison that establishes both orderings
over all nonnegative constant learning rates and deterministic stops within a
finite budget. We study two independent binary predictors under squared loss,
with known population centering and scales, zero initialization, a fixed zero
intercept, and one fresh observation per update. For the two signal families,
explicit bounds give positive risk gaps whenever $0 < q \leq 0.04$ and
$1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$, where $q$ is rare prevalence and $M$ is the
observation cap. As prevalence decreases, the gaps persist even though the
budget grows: the expected number of rare occurrences remains roughly between
one and four. Relative feature scaling therefore remains consequential after
both methods have optimized their rate and stop.

The analysis explains this result through the noise cost of learning each
signal. To learn a dominant rare signal, unscaled training also introduces noise
in the common coefficient. To learn a dominant common signal, standardization
introduces noise in the rare coefficient. Exact moments separate these costs
from incomplete learning and variation due to feature sampling. We first use
fixed-rate comparisons to illustrate the tradeoff and the need for oracle
tuning, then prove the opposite orderings and explore intermediate signal
allocations numerically. We also replace the common feature with several
independent common features while holding their total squared signal fixed.
Their combined error and the rare-coefficient error still satisfy an exact
two-moment recurrence. Numerical comparisons retain both orderings with up to 64
common features.

A controlled study then asks how these comparisons change when training reuses
observations, estimates preprocessing, or fits an intercept. We retain the two
illustrative signal pairs and find that both orderings persist when each
method's tuning choices are fixed before evaluation. Selecting a predictor after
observing training can reverse an ordering, even with exact population risk. We
then examine further obstacles to practical comparisons: missing rare validation
observations, sensitivity to the tuning search, and prediction clipping.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}

Preconditioning changes statistical behavior as well as learning speed. Its
effect on early-stopped predictions can persist even when the least-squares
optimum is unchanged \citep{wadia2021whitening}. Its benefit can depend on which
features carry the signal, even after tuning the stopping time.
\citet{amari2020preconditioning} establish this dependence for aggregate
prediction bias. \citet{su2024preconditioning} study preconditioning for
fresh-sample SGD, comparing the aggregate excess risk of tail-averaged iterates
with ridge regression.

Evaluating prediction within a rare subgroup also changes how coefficient errors
contribute to risk. Conditioning the test law on rare presence while retaining
the training law connects our objective to work on target-distribution risk and
covariate shift \citep{lei2021shift, wu2022pretraining, liu2025precondition}. We
ask which normalization achieves lower rare-subgroup risk after each method
chooses its nonnegative constant learning rate and deterministic stopping time
under the same observation cap. For final iterates of fresh-sample SGD, our
theorem establishes opposite positive risk gaps on two fixed signal families.
\Cref{sec:comparison-proofs} gives detailed comparisons with prior work and
discusses the established mechanisms supporting our analysis.

\section{Normalization, learning, and rare-subgroup risk}
\label{sec:conference-setting}

We isolate the effect of prevalence with two independent binary features: a
common feature $I$ with $\Prob(I = 1) = 1/2$ and a rare feature $J$ with
$\Prob(J = 1) = q$, where $0 < q < 1/2$. For the centered vector
$x = (I - 1/2,J - q)^\top$, suppose
\[
  y = x^\top\beta^* + \varepsilon,\qquad \E(\varepsilon \mid x)
    = 0,\qquad \E(\varepsilon^2 \mid x)
    = \sigma^2.
\]
Write $a = 1/4$, $b = q(1 - q)$, and $C = \E(xx^\top) = \diag(a,b)$. We use
population centering, fix the centered intercept at zero, and initialize
$\beta_0 = 0$.

\paragraph{Scaling and the training budget.}

We divide each feature by a positive scale factor, collected in the diagonal
matrix $S$. Training on $z = S^{-1}x$ with parameter $\theta = S\beta$ gives the
following SGD update on the original coefficient scale:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{k+1}
  = \beta_k
    - \eta S^{-2}x_k(x_k^\top\beta_k - y_k). \label{eq:conference-update}
\end{equation}
We use squared loss with a factor $1/2$. Each update uses one independent fresh
observation, so an observation cap $M$ permits at most $M$ updates. We compare
unscaled features (U), with $P_U = I_2$, and standard-deviation scaling (S, or
SD), with $P_S = \diag(a^{-1},b^{-1})$, where $P = S^{-2}$. Both procedures use
the final iterate.

We can compensate exactly for global rescaling by adjusting the learning rate.
Replacing $S$ by $gS$ and $\eta$ by $g^2\eta$ leaves every update unchanged when
the methods use the same data stream. Changing the relative feature scales,
however, generally changes $\eta S^{-2}$ in a way that a single learning rate
cannot undo. Whether this leads to a risk difference after tuning requires a
separate comparison.

\paragraph{Prediction where the rare feature is present.}

Our main objective is excess prediction risk where the rare feature is present,
$J = 1$. Under half squared loss, independence gives a weighted sum of the two
coefficient mean squared errors:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    R_{R,k} & = \frac18\E\bigl[(\beta_{C,k} - \beta_C^*)^2\bigr]                    \\
            & \quad + \frac{(1 - q)^2}{2}\E\bigl[(\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2\bigr].
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:conference-rare-risk}
\end{equation}
The expectations average over training randomness, and the risk excludes
irreducible test noise. Aggregate excess risk $R_k$ replaces $(1 - q)^2$ by
$q(1 - q)$ in this expression. Thus aggregate error can give little weight to an
error that matters within the rare subgroup. We evaluate both objectives on the
same trained predictor and retain the original training distribution.

\paragraph{Learning signal and fitting noise.}

Let $e_k = \E(\beta_k - \beta^*)$ and
$V_k = \E\bigl((\beta_k - \beta^*)(\beta_k - \beta^*)^\top\bigr)$. Under fresh
sampling, the error second moment decomposes as
\begin{equation}
  V_k = e_ke_k^\top + A_k + \sigma^2N_k, \label{eq:conference-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $A_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance of the noiseless process under random
feature sampling and $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance contributed by
observation noise. This separates incomplete learning from the two sources of
training variation. \Cref{sec:comparison-proofs} derives the exact recurrence.

The mean error obeys $e_{k+1,j} = (1 - \eta C_{jj}/s_j^2)e_{k,j}$. SD scaling
therefore gives every coordinate the multiplier $1 - \eta$. For
$0 < \eta \leq 1$, early stopping gives each coefficient the same shrinkage
factor in expectation, although its uncertainty still depends on feature
sampling and observation noise.

To expose the tradeoff, temporarily fix $\eta_U = 4h$ and $\eta_S = h$. The
common coefficient then has the same mean trajectory under both rules. SD
scaling changes the contraction factor for the mean rare-coefficient error from
$1 - 4hb$ to $1 - h$, while increasing the observation-noise variance added to
that coefficient at each update from $16h^2b\sigma^2$ to $h^2\sigma^2/b$. It
learns the rare signal faster but also introduces more noise.

At a fixed calibration, SD can have lower rare risk early and higher risk later,
as observation noise overtakes its learning advantage. Separate rate and
stopping adjustment on a finite grid removes this reversal in the example of
\cref{sec:conference-comparisons,tab:conference-adjustment}. That example
illustrates the tradeoff, but does not establish a difference after tuning. We
now allow each method to choose any nonnegative constant rate and any
deterministic stop within its budget.

\section{Opposite risk orderings after oracle tuning}
\label{sec:conference-predictive}

We now compare the lowest attainable rare-presence risks of the two scaling
rules in the independent model of \cref{sec:conference-setting}. The budget is
chosen so that rare observations remain scarce: $1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$ gives
roughly one to four rare occurrences in expectation at the cap.

A global learning rate moves both coordinate steps together, while normalization
fixes their ratio. Tuning can change how much each method learns, but cannot
adjust the two steps independently. Lowering the rate reduces observation noise
while slowing learning; stopping sooner also leaves more signal unlearned. The
observation cap limits how far a longer run can compensate for a smaller rate.
The best choice can therefore accept incomplete learning to avoid noise in the
other coefficient.

\paragraph{The oracle comparison.}

Each method chooses its own constant learning rate and iteration count. Let
$\alpha$ denote the rare-coordinate step on the original coefficient scale, so
the update matrices are
\begin{equation}
  D_U(\alpha) = \alpha I_2,\qquad D_S(\alpha)
              = \diag(4b\alpha,\alpha),\quad \alpha
              \geq 0. \label{eq:conference-oracle-rates}
\end{equation}
Here $D = \eta P$, so $\eta_U = \alpha$ and $\eta_S = b\alpha$. Each
parameterization covers every nonnegative constant learning rate for its rule.
At the same $\alpha$, the methods give the rare coefficient the same mean
progress, while SD's common-coordinate step is $4b$ times U's. This complements
the comparison in \cref{sec:conference-setting}, which matched common-coordinate
progress and exposed SD's extra rare-coordinate noise. To learn a dominant rare
signal, U also introduces common-coordinate noise that SD can reduce. To learn a
dominant common signal, SD introduces rare-coordinate noise that U can reduce by
leaving the small rare effect largely unchanged. The oracle chooses $\alpha$
separately for each method; matching progress only explains the constraint each
search faces.

For an observation cap $M \in \mathbb N$, define
\begin{equation}
  R_A^*(M) = \inf_{\substack{\alpha\geq0\\ k\in\{0,\ldots,M\}}} R_{R,k}^{A}(\alpha),\qquad A
           \in \{U,S\}. \label{eq:conference-oracle-risk}
\end{equation}
The oracle chooses a deterministic rate and stop using population risk averaged
over training randomness. It may stop at the prescribed zero initialization.
Because we evaluate finite stops, the search includes rates that need not be
mean-square stable. The cap $M$ applies to each trajectory; the infimum
describes attainable risk, without charging for the search needed to find a rate
and stop. It is not a comparison under equal total tuning computation.

\begin{theorem}[Opposite oracle rare-risk orderings]
  \label{thm:conference-predictive}
  Suppose $\sigma^2 > 0$, $0 < q \leq 0.04$, and
  $1 \leq \tau = Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$ in the independent binary model above.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $(\beta_R^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_C^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_U^*(M) - R_S^*(M) \geq \sigma^2/1000$.
    \item If $(\beta_C^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_R^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_S^*(M) - R_U^*(M) \geq \sigma^2/50$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Either sign is allowed for each coefficient. Both inequalities also hold if
  each rule searches only its mean-square-stable nonnegative constant rates,
  together with initialization.
\end{theorem}

We prove the theorem in \cref{sec:predictive-proofs} by bounding one method
below at every rate and stop and bounding the other above with a stable
candidate. The diagonal error moments satisfy an exact positive recurrence, even
though the stochastic updates couple the independent features. These bounds
compare the best attainable risks without identifying the minimizing rates and
stops.

The prevalence-dependent bounds in \cref{sec:predictive-finite-bounds} give
larger gaps in parts of this range. For example, at $q = 0.02$ and $M = 102$,
they give gaps greater than $0.009\sigma^2$ and $0.064\sigma^2$ for the
respective full signal families. \Cref{sec:predictive-certification} gives a
wider prevalence range for the rare-signal family and stronger small-prevalence
constants. These sufficient cutoffs are not transition boundaries.

The expected number of rare occurrences at the cap is $Mq = \tau/(1 - q)$. Hence
the positive gaps persist as the budget grows inversely with prevalence while
rare observations remain scarce. The interval $\tau \in[1,4]$ describes the
budgets covered by the proof, not a general threshold for learning rare
features. Likewise, the two signal families give sufficient conditions for
opposite orderings, without locating a general transition between them. The
guarantee concerns constant rates, deterministic stops, and population
preprocessing; it leaves batch selection, learning-rate schedules, and
data-dependent selection open.

\paragraph{The compromise after tuning.}

\Cref{fig:tuned-decomposition} separates the risk components at the choices used
in \cref{fig:conference-tuned-intro} for one observation cap. With a dominant
rare signal, U incurs common-coordinate observation noise, while SD keeps that
contribution small and learns more of the rare signal. With a dominant common
signal, SD accepts substantial incomplete learning of that signal together with
rare-coordinate noise. U instead learns the common signal while leaving the
small rare effect largely unchanged. Thus the disadvantaged method need not have
noise as its largest risk component: tuning can favor leaving signal unlearned
to avoid the noise cost of further progress.

\paragraph{Intermediate signal allocations.}

\Cref{fig:signal-allocation} holds the data model fixed and varies how the
squared coefficient magnitude is allocated between the two features. The
finite-grid comparison shows a transition between the orderings whose location
depends on both budget and noise. At each plotted noise level, increasing the
budget lowers the rare-signal share needed for SD to have lower grid-selected
risk. This exploration supplies context for the two sufficient-condition
families; it does not prove an oracle transition outside them.

\subsection{Adding common features}
\label{sec:common-block}

Adding common features gives training more coefficients in which to fit noise.
To examine this effect, replace $I$ with $m$ independent binary features
$I_1,\ldots,I_m$, each with prevalence $1/2$ and independent of $J$. Retain the
remaining population assumptions and write $s = \sum_{j=1}^m(\beta_j^*)^2$ and
$r = (\beta_R^*)^2$. We hold $s$ and $r$ fixed as $m$ increases, so the
comparison changes dimension without adding signal.

The common features enter rare risk through their combined error. Define
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    G_k & = \frac14\sum_{j=1}^m\E[(\beta_{j,k} - \beta_j^*)^2], \\
    H_k & = \E[(\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2].
  \end{aligned}
\]
Then $R_{R,k} = \{G_k + (1 - q)^2H_k\}/2$. With the rare-coordinate step
$\alpha$ used above, set $u = \alpha/4$ for U or $u = b\alpha$ for S, and
$D_b = 1 - b\alpha(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha)$. The exact update is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    G_{k+1} & = (1 - 2u + mu^2)G_k + mu^2(bH_k + \sigma^2), \\
    H_{k+1} & = D_bH_k + b\alpha^2(G_k + \sigma^2),
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:common-block-moments}
\end{equation}
starting from $(G_0,H_0) = (s/4,r)$. The factors of $m$ show how additional
common features increase the noise cost, even if their true coefficients are
zero. The recurrence also implies that increasing $m$ cannot lower either
method's oracle risk at fixed $s$ and $r$. This comparison does not determine
which method has lower risk. \Cref{sec:common-block-details} derives the
recurrence and proves the dimension comparison.

Both orderings persist in all 126 numerical comparisons over seven dimensions,
three prevalences, three caps, and the two signal allocations. Each scaling
searches its own finite grid of stable rates and every integer stop within the
cap. \Cref{fig:common-block} shows the slice with roughly two expected rare
observations. When the rare signal dominates and $q = 10^{-4}$, increasing $m$
from one to 64 raises U's selected risk from $0.256$ to $0.500$, while SD's
changes from $0.229$ to $0.230$. When the common signal dominates, U retains its
advantage, though that advantage narrows with dimension, especially at the
larger prevalence. These comparisons extend the numerical evidence to more
features; they do not extend the uniform gaps in
\cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

\section{From oracle tuning to validation selection}
\label{sec:conference-experiments}

The oracle fixes its rate and stop before observing training. Validation instead
selects among fitted predictors. We first examine this distinction under
controlled changes to the theorem's setup, then consider further obstacles to
practical comparisons.

\subsection{Controlled departures from the oracle model}
\label{sec:theory-bridge}

We retain the independent binary model, coefficient pairs $(0.1,1)$ and
$(1,0.1)$, and noise variance one. At $q = .02$ and $.005$, caps give roughly
one, two, or four expected rare occurrences. Eight training arms separately
introduce observation reuse, estimated preprocessing, and intercept fitting,
then combine them. Reuse draws from 256 noisy training rows. Empirical
preprocessing uses all 256 rows, so its data requirements can exceed the SGD
observation cap. \Cref{sec:theory-bridge-protocol} gives the full protocol.

For each scaling and training arm, we calibrate a rate factor and stop to
minimize mean rare risk, then evaluate those fixed choices on independent
training runs. With estimated preprocessing, the factor determines a constant
rate from the training data. Both orderings persist in all 96 comparisons: SD
has lower rare risk for the dominant rare signal, and U has lower rare risk for
the dominant common signal (\cref{fig:bridge-controlled}). This evidence
concerns the tested signal pairs and a conservative finite rate search; it does
not extend the theorem to these training procedures.

Selecting among the fitted predictors can reverse an ordering.
\Cref{tab:bridge-selection} compares calibration, rare validation loss, and
exact population rare risk on the same candidate paths. Both forms of selection
after training favor U in this example, while calibration favors SD. Every
validation set here contains rare observations. The reversal under exact
selection shows that validation noise is not needed: choosing the best predictor
in each training run is a different objective from choosing the best tuning
settings on average. Exact selection reduces U's rare risk by $.2771$ and SD's
by $.1457$; U's larger gain overturns SD's advantage under calibration.

\subsection{Further practical comparisons}

We now examine further binary laws and a real dataset, focusing on missing rare
validation observations, sensitivity to the tuning search, and sensitivity to
prediction postprocessing.

These studies sample with replacement from fixed noisy training sets, estimate
preprocessing, and include fitted intercepts. Reusing a row also reuses its
noise, and the processed-observation count $M$ can exceed the training-set size
$n$. Unlike \cref{sec:theory-bridge}, these studies also change the signal laws
and candidate searches, so they do not isolate departures from the theorem. The
source supplement contains the full protocols, code, data, and saved results
(\cref{sec:practical,sec:reproducibility}).

\paragraph{Scaling rules.}

Alongside U and SD, we include damping to limit amplification of low-variance
features and variance scaling to examine stronger amplification. Let $v_j$ be
the population feature variance or its training-data estimate. Damping uses a
fixed offset $\lambda > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  s_j = \sqrt{v_j + \lambda},\qquad P_{jj}
      = \frac{1}{v_j + \lambda}. \label{eq:conference-damped}
\end{equation}
We use $\lambda = 0.01$ as a heuristic, fixed before evaluation. At the same
learning rate, it approximately halves the SD update at binary prevalence $0.01$
while retaining $0.96$ of the update for a balanced feature. Variance scaling
uses $s_j = v_j$, giving $P_{jj} = v_j^{-2}$ for positive variances. These
additional rules examine how the amount of amplification affects the comparison;
the theorem does not establish an optimal choice among them.

\paragraph{Missing rare validation observations.}

The sampled binary study uses eight laws, 128 replications per law, 256 training
observations, and 128 validation observations. It compares known preprocessing,
estimated scales, training centering with a fixed or fitted intercept, and
uncentered predictors with a fitted intercept. Rates are calibrated by the
largest empirical curvature, which does not guarantee stochastic stability.
\Cref{sec:practical,sec:practical-searches} give the laws and candidate
searches.

For the two laws with $q = .002$, there are no rare validation observations in
106 and 105 of 128 replications. The declared selection rule then falls back to
aggregate loss. One selected SD predictor has validation half squared loss
$2.086$ but rare loss about $2.257 \times 10^{33}$. High-precision replay of the
worst selected replication in each combination of law and scaling preserves
those selections in the five-rate search (\cref{sec:five-rate-replay}). This is
a coverage failure for the admitted candidate family: validation can select a
predictor whose extreme errors occur in a group absent from validation. It does
not show that such losses persist under a stability-screened search or arise
from the stable tradeoff in the theorem.

The 25-rate synthetic search has unresolved replay discrepancies affecting
extreme mean risks. We do not use those means to rank normalization rules;
\cref{tab:review-audit-impact} identifies the affected summaries and separates
their evidence from the five-rate high-precision investigation.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to tuning in categorical prediction.}

We next examine how the tuning search and selection objective affect a
comparison on UCI Mushroom. We predict the recorded poisonous label under
squared loss using all 22 categorical predictors \citep{uciMushroom}. The
declared rare subgroup contains the 72 green-spore-print records among 8124
observations. One-hot vocabularies, means, and scales use training data alone;
missingness is a category and unknown categories map to zero blocks. We use 32
paired partitions at each training/validation size, $(256,128)$ and
$(2048,1024)$, and test on the remaining records. MCSEs describe variation
across partitions and optimization within this fixed dataset, not population
confidence intervals.

Each of the four rules receives the same 25 curvature-relative rate factors from
$10^{-5}$ to $409.6$, batches $B \in \{16,128\}$, and stopping checkpoints
$0,512,2048,8192$. Each selects its rate, batch, and stop by aggregate or rare
validation loss. At cap $M$, the 50 trajectories cost up to $50M$ processed
training observations per rule and preprocessing arm; scoring, preprocessing,
and curvature calibration are additional work. The damping offset remains fixed
at $.01$. \Cref{tab:search-protocols} documents this search and the smaller
grids used to assess tuning sensitivity.

\Cref{fig:conference-practical} shows how the search and clipping affect the
comparison at the larger sample size. Under aggregate selection, the SD-minus-U
rare-risk gap changes from $-.006570$ (paired MCSE $.001526$) in the five-rate
search to $-.001192$ ($.001318$) in the 25-rate search. The paired change is
$+.005379$ ($.001061$). The expanded search therefore reduces the SD advantage
and leaves the gap within two MCSE of zero, without establishing equivalence.
Under rare selection, the gap changes from $-.009307$ ($.001315$) to $-.001421$
($.000470$), a paired change of $+.007886$ ($.001247$). SD retains lower rare
loss in this comparison, but its raw aggregate loss varies widely. Both the
search and the selection objective matter when interpreting the comparison.

Damping has lower aggregate and rare loss than no scaling under aggregate
selection, with differences $-.000532$ ($.000056$) and $-.005273$ ($.000797$).

more total work and does not establish a deployable offset-selection rule
(\cref{sec:mushroom-sensitivity}).

Across both sample sizes and the standard and refined stopping searches, no
positive-budget Mushroom selection reaches a rate boundary. This resolves the
tested grid edges, not continuous optimality. At the smaller sample size, five
training partitions and thirteen validation partitions lack green observations;
we retain them with their declared fallbacks.

The experiment measures held-out prediction differences in a rank-deficient
one-hot representation. It does not isolate transient learning from solution
selection, so these differences cannot be attributed to early stopping alone. A
converged prediction reference and identifiable prediction contrasts would be
needed for that interpretation. Random partitions of these field-guide
descriptions also do not establish generalization to new species or field data.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to prediction clipping.}

For labels in $[0,1]$, clipping predictions to this interval cannot increase
squared loss for any fixed predictor. This provides a further check on how much
the observed comparisons depend on extreme predictions.
\Cref{fig:conference-practical} shows the aggregate losses under rare selection;
\cref{tab:review-clipping} reports both losses under both selection objectives.
Clipping retains the original validation scores and requires no additional
training.

Under rare validation, clipping changes the aggregate mean ordering of SD and
unscaled training and sharply reduces variance scaling's aggregate loss. Damping
retains the lowest mean of both losses among the four rules under either
selection objective. These are descriptive comparisons of the clipped
predictors. Their changed magnitudes show why raw catastrophic losses cannot
support general recommendations about normalization. Selecting by clipped
validation loss would define a different procedure, which this post hoc
diagnostic does not evaluate.

\section{Discussion}

Normalization determines how strongly a finite-budget learner responds to the
evidence for each feature. Our oracle comparison shows that relative scaling can
change the lowest attainable rare-subgroup risk even after each method has
optimized its constant learning rate and deterministic stopping time. Which
scaling has lower risk depends on the signal: learning a rare effect can justify
amplifying scarce observations, while learning a common effect can favor leaving
a small rare effect largely unchanged. The positive gaps persist as prevalence
decreases while rare observations remain scarce.

The theorem establishes this conclusion for two independent binary features,
known population preprocessing, zero initialization, a fixed zero intercept,
fresh observations, and the stated signal and budget ranges. The numerical
exploration places the two signal families in a broader picture of budget and
noise, without proving a general transition boundary. Learning-rate schedules,
batch selection, data-dependent stopping, and adaptive optimizers remain outside
the comparison.

Replacing the common feature with an independent block preserves the exact
moment reduction. At fixed total signal strength, increasing the number of
common features cannot lower either method's oracle risk. The numerical
comparison retains both orderings through 64 common features, with dimension
changing the size of the gaps. Uniform separation bounds for this larger model
remain open.

The controlled study shows that both orderings can survive changes to the
training procedure. They persist for the tested signal pairs under observation
reuse, estimated preprocessing, and intercept fitting when tuning choices are
fixed before evaluation. Selecting a predictor after observing training can
reverse an ordering, even with exact population risk. An extension to adaptive
selection would therefore need to account for the benefit each scaling gains
from choosing among its realized predictors.

The further practical studies show that expanded tuning can remove an apparent
scaling advantage, missing validation groups can conceal extreme errors, and
clipping can change empirical orderings without further training. Together,
these findings make the selection procedure and candidate family part of the
statistical comparison rather than incidental implementation choices.

Feature normalization therefore belongs in the statistical specification of
training under a finite budget: even in a two-feature model, tuning a global
learning rate and stopping time need not make different relative scalings
equivalent in rare-subgroup risk.

\section{Proof of the comparison after oracle tuning}
\label{sec:predictive-proofs}

We prove \cref{thm:conference-predictive} directly at positive prevalence. The
bounds below are explicit functions of $q$ and $\tau = Mq(1 - q)$. Finite minima
over rate intervals bound the continuous search; they do not restrict the oracle
to a rate grid. Analytic bounds cover every rate above one. The checker
\texttt{checks/predictive\_fixed\_q.py} certifies the reported positive regions
with rational arithmetic and checks the moment bounds against observation-level
tensor calculations.

The source supplement also includes small-prevalence checks in
\path{checks/predictive_theorem.py} and \path{checks/predictive_review.py}.

\subsection{Exact moments}

By homogeneity, take $\sigma^2 = 1$. Put $s = (\beta_C^*)^2$,
$r = (\beta_R^*)^2$, $b = q(1 - q)$, and $w = (1 - q)^2$. Write
$C_k = \E\bigl((\beta_{C,k} - \beta_C^*)^2\bigr)$ and
$H_k = \E\bigl((\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2\bigr)$. Independence gives
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k} = C_k/8 + wH_k/2. \label{eq:predictive-risk}
\end{equation}
The required feature moments are $x_C^2 = 1/4$, $\E(x_R^2) = b$,
$\E(x_R^4) = b(1 - 3b)$, and $\E(x_C^2x_R^2) = b/4$. Mixed moments with an odd
power of $x_C$ vanish. Expanding the squared update and using the conditional
noise assumptions gives
\begin{equation}
  \binom{C_{k+1}}{H_{k+1}} = \begin{pmatrix}
                               A           & 4u^2b \\
                               b\alpha^2/4 & D_b
                             \end{pmatrix}\binom{C_k}{H_k}
                             + \binom{4u^2}{b\alpha^2},\qquad \binom{C_0}{H_0}
                           = \binom{s}{r}, \label{eq:predictive-moments}
\end{equation}
where $A = (1 - u)^2$, $D_b = 1 - b\alpha(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha)$, and
$u = \alpha/4$ for U or $u = b\alpha$ for S. The diagonal equations and risk do
not involve cross error moments, so coefficient signs do not affect risk. Every
matrix entry is nonnegative for every $\alpha \geq 0$: in particular,
$D_b = (1 - b\alpha)^2 + b\alpha^2(1 - 4b) \geq 0$. Increasing either initial
squared coefficient cannot reduce risk.

For $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, the row sums are
\[
  1 - u\{2 - u(1 + 4b)\} < 1,\qquad 1 - b\alpha\{2 - 5\alpha/4 + 3b\alpha\} < 1.
\]
The homogeneous moments contract and the noise moments stay bounded. The cross
moment is bounded by $\sqrt{C_kH_k}$, so all positive candidate rates used below
are mean-square stable. At zero rate, the predictor stays at initialization.

\subsection{Explicit finite-prevalence bounds}
\label{sec:predictive-finite-bounds}

Throughout, $0 < q \leq 1/16$ and $1 \leq \tau \leq 4$. Let $N = 4096$ and
$a_j = j/N$, for $j = 0,\ldots,N$. Define
\[
  \begin{aligned}
    d_b(a) & = a(2 - a + 3ba),                                                            \\
    n_j    & = \min\left\{ w,\frac{wa_j}{2 - a_j + 3ba_j} + \frac{a_j}{8 - a_j} \right\}, \\
    p_j    & = \min\left\{ \frac18,\frac{wa_j}{2(2 - ba_j)} \right\},
  \end{aligned}
\]
and
\[
  z_{R,j} = \exp\left\{ -\frac{\tau d_b(a_{j+1})}
                                  {1 - bd_b(a_{j+1})} \right\},\qquad z_{C,j}
          = \exp\left\{ -\frac{2\tau a_{j+1}}{1 - ba_{j+1}} \right\}.
\]
Define the lower bounds
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    L_R(q,\tau) & = \min\left\{ \frac{w}{2(1 + 3b)}, \min_{0 \leq j<N}\frac{n_j + (w - n_j)z_{R,j}}2 \right\}, \\
    L_C(q,\tau) & = \min_{0 \leq j<N}\left\{ p_j + \left( \frac18 - p_j \right)z_{C,j} \right\}.
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-finite-lower}
\end{equation}
The integer $N$ controls only the precision of the lower bounds. All their
denominators are positive on the stated domain.

For the upper bounds, use $k = M$ and the stable rates
\[
  \alpha_S = \frac{2}{\tau + 4},\qquad \alpha_U = \frac{8b}{\tau} = \frac8M.
\]
Put
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    p_S = \frac{\alpha_S(1 - q)}{2 - (1 - q)\alpha_S},\qquad G = \frac{\alpha_U}{2 - \alpha_U/4}, \\
    H_* = \frac{1/100 + b\alpha_U/(2 - \alpha_U/4) + \tau\alpha_U^2(1 + G/4)}
               {1 - \tau b\alpha_U^2G/4}.
  \end{gathered}
\]
Here $\alpha_S \leq 2/5$, $\alpha_U \leq 1/2$, and
$\tau b\alpha_U^2G/4 \leq 1/960$, so $H_*$ is well defined. Define
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    V_S(q,\tau) & = \frac12\left[ p_S + \left( w + \frac1{400} - p_S \right) e^{-\tau\alpha_S\{2-(1-q)\alpha_S\}} \right], \\
    V_U(q,\tau) & = \frac{e^{-4}}8 + \frac G8 + \left( \frac w2 + \frac{bG}8 \right)H_*.
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-finite-upper}
\end{equation}
Define the gap bounds
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_R(q,\tau) = L_R(q,\tau) - V_S(q,\tau),\qquad \Delta_C(q,\tau)
                   = L_C(q,\tau)
                     - V_U(q,\tau). \label{eq:predictive-finite-gaps}
\end{equation}
For the rare-signal and common-signal families, respectively, we will prove
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    R_U^*(M) - R_S^*(M) & \geq \Delta_R(q,\tau), \\
    R_S^*(M) - R_U^*(M) & \geq \Delta_C(q,\tau),
  \end{aligned} \label{eq:predictive-oracle-gaps}
\end{equation}
throughout $0 < q \leq 1/16$ and $1 \leq \tau \leq 4$, with $\sigma^2 = 1$ as
above. For general positive noise variance, multiply both bounds by $\sigma^2$.
The bounds need not be positive everywhere on this domain. A nonpositive bound
does not establish the opposite ordering.

\subsection{Dominant rare signal}

Suppose $r = 1$ and $s \leq 1/100$. For U and $\alpha \in[0,1]$,
\[
  D_b - A = \frac{(1 - 4b)\alpha}{16} \{8 + (12b - 1)\alpha\} \geq 0.
\]
Dropping nonnegative coupling terms in \cref{eq:predictive-moments} and summing
the direct noise terms gives, with $z = D_b^k$,
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k}^U \geq \frac12\left[ wz + \left\{ \frac{w\alpha}{2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{8 - \alpha} \right\}(1 - z) \right]. \label{eq:predictive-u-lower}
\end{equation}
For the common-noise term, use $1 - A^k \geq 1 - D_b^k$. The formula extends
directly to zero rate. Its right side is affine in $z$. Taking the smaller value
at $z = 1$ or $z = D_b^M$ is equivalent to capping the noise coefficient at $w$.

For $0 \leq x < 1$, the logarithm series gives $-\log(1 - x) \leq x/(1 - x)$.
Consequently,
\[
  D_b^M \geq \exp\left\{ -\frac{\tau d_b(\alpha)}
                                      {1 - bd_b(\alpha)} \right\}.
\]
Both $d_b(\alpha)$ and the noise coefficient increase on $[0,1]$. For
$\alpha \in[a_j,a_{j+1}]$, use the left endpoint for the capped noise
coefficient and the right endpoint for the exponential lower bound. This gives
the $j$th term in $L_R$ for every $0 \leq k \leq M$.

For every $\alpha \geq 1$, put $m_b = 1/(1 + 3b)$. The identity
\[
  D_bm_b + b\alpha^2 - m_b = \frac{2b\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{1 + 3b} \geq 0
\]
and positivity of the recurrence imply $H_k \geq m_b$ by induction from
$H_0 = 1$. Thus $R_{R,k}^U \geq w/\{2(1 + 3b)\}$ at every finite stop, including
zero. This proves the remaining term in $L_R$, without a stability restriction.

For the S candidate, retain the exact risk weight by setting
$W_k = C_k/4 + wH_k = 2R_{R,k}^S$. Let
$\rho = 1 - b\alpha\{2 - (1 - q)\alpha\}$. The recurrence gives
\[
  W_{k+1} \leq \rho W_k + b\alpha^2(1 - q),
\]
because the common coefficient has multiplier $\rho$ and
\[
  \rho w - (wD_b + b^3\alpha^2) = 2wbq(1 - 2q)\alpha^2 \geq 0.
\]
At $\alpha = \alpha_S$, $0 < \rho < 1$ and $W_0 \leq w + 1/400$. Geometric
summation therefore bounds the risk by
\[
  \frac12\left[ p_S + (w + 1/400 - p_S)\rho^M \right] \leq V_S(q,\tau).
\]
The last step uses $\rho^M \leq e^{-\tau\alpha_S\{2-(1-q)\alpha_S\}}$ and
$w + 1/400 > p_S$. Hence every U rate and stop exceeds this S candidate's risk
by at least $\Delta_R(q,\tau)$, uniformly over the rare-signal family.

\subsection{Dominant common signal}

Suppose $s = 1$ and $r \leq 1/100$. Under S, put $A = (1 - b\alpha)^2$. Since
$D_b - A = b\alpha^2(1 - 4b) \geq 0$, positivity gives
\[
  C_k \geq A^k,\qquad H_k
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}D_b^j
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A^j.
\]
For $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, geometric summation yields
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \frac{A^k}{8} + \frac{w\alpha}{2(2 - b\alpha)}(1 - A^k).
\]
The expression is affine in $A^k$. Minimizing over $k \leq M$ caps its second
coefficient at $1/8$ and replaces $A^k$ by $A^M$. Moreover,
\[
  A^M \geq \exp\left\{ -\frac{2\tau\alpha}{1 - b\alpha} \right\}.
\]
The plateau and the magnitude of the exponent both increase with $\alpha$. Their
left and right endpoints give the $j$th term in $L_C$ on each rate interval.
Zero rate gives risk at least $1/8$ directly.

For $\alpha \geq 1$, if $A \geq 1$ then $C_k \geq 1$ and the risk is at least
$1/8$. Otherwise $0 < b\alpha < 2$ and the same geometric bound gives
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \min\left\{ \frac18, \frac{w\alpha}{2(2 - b\alpha)} \right\}
            \geq \min\left\{ \frac18,\frac{w}{2(2 - b)} \right\}
            = \frac18.
\]
The last equality follows from $q \leq 1/16$. Since $L_C \leq 1/8$, the bound
covers all larger rates as well.

For the U candidate, set $\alpha = \alpha_U$, $u = \alpha/4$, and
$H_{\max} = \max_{0 \leq j \leq M}H_j$. Summing the common equation gives
\[
  C_j \leq A^j + G(1 + bH_{\max}).
\]
Since $D_b \leq 1$, drop the rare-coordinate contraction and sum up to any
$j \leq M$. Using $\sum_{j \geq 0}A^j = 1/\{u(2 - u)\}$ gives
\[
  H_{\max} \leq \frac1{100}
                + \frac{b\alpha}{2 - \alpha/4}
                + \tau\alpha^2\left( 1 + \frac G4 \right)
                + \frac{\tau b\alpha^2G}{4}H_{\max}.
\]
Thus $H_{\max} \leq H_*$. Also,
$A^M = (1 - \alpha/4)^{2M} \leq e^{-\alpha M/2} = e^{-4}$, which gives
$R_{R,M}^U \leq V_U(q,\tau)$. Every S rate and stop therefore exceeds this U
candidate's risk by at least $\Delta_C(q,\tau)$, uniformly over the
common-signal family.

Taking the two oracle infima proves \cref{eq:predictive-oracle-gaps}.
Restricting each search to stable rates preserves both lower bounds and both
candidates. Rescaling by $\sigma^2$ gives the bounds for general positive noise
variance. The certificates below then imply \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.
The argument requires neither stability of the losing method's rate nor
attainment of either infimum.

\subsection{Positive regions and the small-prevalence corollary}
\label{sec:predictive-certification}

The checker certifies the following lower bounds for every $1 \leq \tau \leq 4$.
All intervals include every positive prevalence up to the displayed endpoint.
The bounds apply to the entire respective signal families.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}llrr@{}}
    \toprule

Here is the full certification rule. On a box
$[a_0,a_1] \times[q_0,q_1] \times[t_0,t_1]$, set $b_i = q_i(1 - q_i)$ and
$w_i = (1 - q_i)^2$. For the rare lower bound, use $w_1$, the capped noise
coefficient at $(a_0,q_1)$, and the exponential lower bound at $(a_1,q_1,t_1)$.
For the common lower bound, use $\min\{1/8,w_1a_0/[2(2 - b_0a_0)]\}$ for the

follow from monotonicity of $b$, $d_b$, the rate plateaus, and the exponents on
the stated ranges. Also include the rare large-rate floor at $q_1$.

The S upper bound decreases with $q$ and $\tau$: its plateau decreases, its
initial coefficient decreases, and its exponent
$4\tau(\tau + 3 + q)/(\tau + 4)^2$ increases. Use $V_S(q_0,t_0)$ on the box. For
the U upper bound, substitute $b_1$ for $b$, $w_0$ for $w$, and $t_0$ for
$\tau$. Indeed, $\alpha_U$, $G$, the numerator of $H_*$, and its feedback
coefficient increase with $b$ and decrease with $\tau$ after substituting
$\alpha_U = 8b/\tau$.

To enclose exponentials, for rational $x \in[0,4]$ put
$P_{18}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{18}x^j/j!$. Positivity and the ratio of successive
omitted terms give
\begin{equation}
  P_{18}(x)
  \leq e^x
  \leq P_{18}(x)
       + \frac{x^{19}/19!}{1 - x/20}. \label{eq:predictive-exp-certificate}
\end{equation}
Taking reciprocals encloses $e^{-x}$. The checker applies this rule to one
fourth of each exponent and raises the endpoints to the fourth power. All
exponents lie in $[0,16]$, so the rule covers every evaluation using rational
arithmetic only.

Start with $[0,1] \times[0,Q] \times[1,4]$ for each table row; the formulas
extend continuously to $q = 0$. Accept a box only if its lower margin reaches
the stated gap. Otherwise bisect the dimension with largest width among
$a_1 - a_0$, $q_1 - q_0$, and $(t_1 - t_0)/3$, breaking ties in that order. Stop
bisecting rate intervals at width $1/N$. All rate endpoints therefore align with
the partition defining \cref{eq:predictive-finite-lower}; an accepted box bounds
every constituent interval's expression. The accepted boxes cover the domain,
and their exact volumes sum to $3Q$. Thus the certificates establish the bounds
over continuous prevalence and budget ranges, including every admissible integer
stop. No sampled rate or prevalence search is used as proof.

At $q = 1/50$ and $M = 102$, direct evaluation of the same formulas with
rational exponential enclosures gives $\Delta_R > 0.0092472$ and
$\Delta_C > 0.0641380$. These are certified family bounds, not estimates of the
oracle gaps. As a separate diagnostic, the checker compares 888 bounds with
80-digit observation-level tensor powers, including zero stops, both signal
families, and rates above the stability range.

\begin{corollary}[Small prevalence]
  \label{cor:predictive-small-q}
  Under the assumptions of \cref{thm:conference-predictive}, if
  $0 < q \leq 10^{-4}$, then the rare-signal gap is at least $11\sigma^2/1000$
  and the common-signal gap is at least $11\sigma^2/200$.
\end{corollary}

The last two rows of the table prove the corollary. The certified gaps grow as
the prevalence range narrows. These are sufficient bounds, not the largest
possible gaps or prevalence ranges: the proof discards nonnegative coupling
terms and replaces discrete powers by exponential bounds.

\section{One rare feature and several common features}
\label{sec:common-block-details}

\subsection{Moment reduction}

Let $I_1,\ldots,I_m$ be independent Bernoulli variables with prevalence $1/2$,
and let $J$ be an independent Bernoulli variable with prevalence $q$, where
$m \geq 1$ and $0 < q < 1/2$. With population-centered features, the response is
\[
  y = \sum_{j=1}^m\beta_j^*(I_j - 1/2)
      + \beta_R^*(J - q)
      + \varepsilon, \qquad \E(\varepsilon \mid x)
    = 0, \qquad \E(\varepsilon^2 \mid x)
    = \sigma^2.
\]
Training uses fresh observations, zero initialization, and a fixed zero
intercept. On the original coefficient scale, the rare-coordinate step is
$\alpha$ and every common-coordinate step is $4u$, with $u = \alpha/4$ for U or
$u = b\alpha$ for S. Here $b = q(1 - q)$, as before. Define
$s = \sum_{j=1}^m(\beta_j^*)^2$, $r = (\beta_R^*)^2$, and
\[
  V_{j,k} = \E[(\beta_{j,k} - \beta_j^*)^2],\qquad G_k
          = \frac14\sum_{j=1}^m V_{j,k},\qquad H_k
          = \E[(\beta_{R,k} - \beta_R^*)^2].
\]

Expanding the squared SGD update for a common coefficient gives
\[
  V_{j,k+1} = (1 - u)^2V_{j,k}
              + u^2\sum_{\substack{\ell=1\\\ell\ne j}}^m V_{\ell,k}
              + 4u^2bH_k
              + 4u^2\sigma^2.
\]
The common feature has second moment $1/4$ and fourth moment $1/16$.
Independence and centering remove terms involving two distinct coefficient
errors from this diagonal equation, even when those errors are correlated.
Summing over $j$ and dividing by four gives the first line of
\cref{eq:common-block-moments}. The rare equation follows from
$\E[(J - q)^4] = b(1 - 3b)$ and the same expansion. Conditioning the test
distribution on $J = 1$ leaves the common features centered and independent, so
the conditional second-moment matrix is diagonal and
$R_{R,k} = \{G_k + (1 - q)^2H_k\}/2$.

The recurrence depends on the common coefficients only through $s$. Thus the
expected rare risk is unchanged if the same squared signal is concentrated in
one common feature or spread across the block. Coefficient signs also have no
effect on this risk. Setting $m = 1$ recovers \cref{eq:predictive-moments}, with
$G_k = C_k/4$ and with the observation-noise terms multiplied by $\sigma^2$.

\subsection{Comparison across dimensions}

For fixed $q,s,r,\sigma^2$, write $R_A^*(M;m)$ for the infimum of rare risk
under scaling $A$ over every nonnegative constant rate and deterministic stop
$k \in \{0,\ldots,M\}$ in the model with $m$ common features.

\begin{proposition}[Increasing the common-feature dimension]
  \label{prop:common-block-dimension}
  For either scaling $A \in \{U,S\}$ and integers $m_2 \geq m_1 \geq 1$,
  \[
    R_A^*(M;m_2) \geq R_A^*(M;m_1).
  \]
  The same inequality holds at each fixed nonnegative rare-coordinate step
  $\alpha$ and stop $k$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
  At fixed $\alpha$, the value of $u$ does not depend on $m$. Every coefficient
  in \cref{eq:common-block-moments} is nonnegative. In particular,
  \[
    1 - 2u + mu^2 = (1 - u)^2 + (m - 1)u^2
                  \geq 0, \qquad D_b
                  = (1 - b\alpha)^2 + b\alpha^2(1 - 4b)
                  \geq 0.
  \]
  Increasing $m$ increases the coefficients and noise term in the common
  equation and leaves the rare equation unchanged. Both systems start at
  $(s/4,r)$, so induction gives coordinatewise larger moments at every stop. The
  risk weights are nonnegative and independent of $m$, proving the fixed-rate
  comparison. Taking the infimum over the same rates and stops proves the oracle
  comparison.
\end{proof}

The proposition applies separately to each method. It does not imply that their
risk difference is monotone in $m$, or that either ordering from the two-feature
theorem persists.

\subsection{Numerical comparisons}
\label{sec:common-block-numerics}

We use $m \in \{1,2,4,8,16,32,64\}$, $q \in \{0.02,0.005,10^{-4}\}$,
$\sigma^2 = 1$, and $(s,r) \in \{(0.01,1),\allowbreak(1,0.01)\}$. The
observation caps are

  \begin{array}{c|rrr}
       q    &     \multicolumn{3}{c}{M}      \\
    \hline
     0.02   &       52 &      102 &      204 \\
     0.005  &      202 &      402 &      804 \\
    10^{-4} & 10{,}002 & 20{,}002 & 40{,}004
  \end{array}
\]
These approximate $Mq(1 - q) = 1,2,4$ while remaining in $[1,4]$. The initial

Each scaling uses zero rate and 1,025 positive rates geometrically spaced
between $10^{-7}$ and $0.999$ times its mean-square stability limit. To compute
that limit, write $u = c\alpha$, where $c = 1/4$ for U or $c = b$ for S, and let
$T$ be the homogeneous transition in \cref{eq:common-block-moments}. Then
\[
  \det(I - T) = bc\alpha^2(4 - L\alpha + K\alpha^2),\qquad L
              = 2(1 - 3b + mc),\qquad K
              = mc(1 - 4b).
\]
The first positive root gives
\[
  \alpha_{\mathrm{stab}} = \frac{8}{L + \sqrt{L^2 - 16K}}.
\]
The search includes every integer stop from zero through each cap. It selects
the rate and stop that minimize expected rare risk, before any training
realization is observed. This finite stable-rate search is narrower than the
unrestricted oracle in \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

preserves every ordering. No positive-stop selection uses either endpoint of the
positive rate grid. These checks measure sensitivity to the tested grid; they do
not certify its distance from the continuous optimum.

\section{Fixed-calibration comparisons and certificates}
\label{sec:conference-comparisons}

These comparisons make the learning--noise tradeoff explicit at prescribed
rates. They support the motivation in \cref{sec:conference-setting}, while the
oracle theorem compares the larger families obtained by optimizing the rate and
stop. We also allow dependence here to examine how aggregate and rare-presence
objectives can disagree.

\paragraph{Mini-batches and the observation budget.}

For batches of $B$ independent fresh observations, the update in
\cref{eq:conference-update} becomes
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{k+1}
  = \beta_k
    - \frac{\eta S^{-2}}{B}\sum_{i=1}^B x_{k,i}(x_{k,i}^\top\beta_k - y_{k,i}). \label{eq:comparison-batch-update}
\end{equation}
Each update averages the $B$ gradients, so $k$ updates use $M = kB$
observations. The main theorem takes $B = 1$.

\paragraph{Allowing dependent binary features.}

Retain the marginal probabilities and centered features of
\cref{sec:conference-setting}, but allow
\[
  \Prob(I = 1,J = 1) = \frac{q(1 + \ell)}{2},\qquad - 1 \leq \ell \leq 1.
\]
For $0 < q < 1/2$, these ranges ensure nonnegative cell probabilities. To
express the risks under dependence, let $C = \E(xx^\top)$ and
$C_G = \E(xx^\top \mid G)$. Then
\begin{equation}
  R_k = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(CV_k),\qquad R_{G,k}
      = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(C_GV_k). \label{eq:conference-risks}
\end{equation}
The conditional second moment $C_G$ includes the contribution of the conditional
feature mean. For rare presence, $G = \{J = 1\}$. With $a = 1/4$,
$b = q(1 - q)$, and $c = q\ell/2$,
\[
  C = \begin{pmatrix}
        a & c \\
        c & b
      \end{pmatrix}, \qquad C_R
    = \begin{pmatrix}
        a             & \ell(1 - q)/2 \\
        \ell(1 - q)/2 & (1 - q)^2
      \end{pmatrix}.
\]
Independence corresponds to $\ell = 0$ and recovers
\cref{eq:conference-rare-risk}. Population preprocessing, zero initialization,
the fixed zero intercept, and fresh sampling remain as in the main text.

In this section, we fix $\eta_U = 4h$ and $\eta_S = h$. Both rules then have
diagonal drift $h$ in the common coordinate. Under independence, this gives the
common coefficient the same mean trajectory for both rules, although dependence
generally breaks the match. We specify $h$ in advance and use it for both
methods.

\subsection{Learning the rare signal versus fitting noise}

Under independence, SD scaling changes the contraction factor for the mean rare
coefficient error from $1 - 4hb$ to $1 - h$. At the same time, it increases the
observation-noise variance added to that coefficient at each update from
$16h^2b\sigma^2/B$ to $h^2\sigma^2/(bB)$. Standardization therefore speeds up
learning while introducing more noise. Random feature sampling contributes
additional variance to both procedures.

The diagonal error moments satisfy a nonnegative two-dimensional recurrence. By
omitting the terms that couple the two coordinates, we obtain a scalar
approximation to rare risk with an explicit nonnegative remainder. Let $F$ and
$N$ denote the noiseless and unit-noise parts of the SD-minus-U risk difference.
The approximation gives bounds $F_- \leq F \leq F_+$ and $N_- \leq N \leq N_+$.
When $F_+ < 0 < N_-$, the noise-variance threshold
$\sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^2 = -F/N$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{-F_+}{N_+} \leq \sigma_{\mathrm{crit}}^2
                   \leq \frac{-F_-}{N_-}. \label{eq:conference-threshold}
\end{equation}
The remainder accounts for the dependence that the sampled updates introduce
between coefficient errors, even when the features are independent. We derive
the approximation and define its terms in \cref{sec:comparison-proofs}.

\begin{theorem}[Stopping and batch reversal]
  \label{thm:conference-c1}
  In the binary model with $\ell = 0$, suppose
  \begin{gather*}
    q \in[0.019,0.021] ,\quad h \in[0.0019,0.0021] ,               \\
    \beta_C^*,\beta_R^* \in[0.95,1.05] ,\quad\sigma^2 \in[1.5,5] .
  \end{gather*}
  Both U and S are mean-square stable for $B \in \{1,10\}$. Their rare-risk
  contrast $\Delta_{B,k} = R_{R,k}^S - R_{R,k}^U$ lies in the intervals in
  \cref{tab:conference-c1} throughout this parameter box.
\end{theorem}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{rrrr}
    \toprule
    $B$ &    $k$ & $M = kB$ &    $\Delta_{B,k}$ \\
    \midrule
      1 &  1,000 &    1,000 &   $[-.353,-.160]$ \\
      1 & 10,000 &   10,000 &    $[.0021,.125]$ \\
     10 &  1,000 &   10,000 &   $[-.386,-.286]$ \\
     10 & 10,000 &  100,000 & $[-.0281,-.0016]$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{
    Bounds on the rare-risk difference in \cref{thm:conference-c1}, valid

    intervals enclose the exact risk differences and do not represent Monte
    Carlo uncertainty.
  }
  \label{tab:conference-c1}
\end{table}

At $B = 1$, the ordering reverses as training continues. With the same budget
$M = 10{,}000$, it also differs between $B = 1$ and $B = 10$. These are
comparisons between normalization rules at each batch size and budget. They do
not establish which batch size is better for either rule.

For the anchor $q = .02$, $h = .002$, $\beta^* = (1,1)^\top$, and
$\sigma^2 = 3$, the early $B = 1$ rare risk is $.356678$ for U and $.090407$ for
S. Squared bias contributes $.353207$ and $.011040$, respectively, so the early
advantage comes mainly from learning the rare signal more quickly. By
$k = 10{,}000$, U has learned enough of that signal to reach risk $.028145$,
whereas S has risk $.078788$, almost entirely due to observation noise. The
exact calculation includes variation from feature sampling at every step.

\subsection{Aggregate and rare-presence objectives}

Dependence can make the two prediction objectives favor different rules. Let
$\Delta V = V_k^S - V_k^U$. Direct substitution into \cref{eq:conference-risks}
gives
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_R - \Delta_A = \frac{1 - 2q}{2}\bigl\{(1 - q)\Delta V_{RR} + \ell\Delta V_{CR}\bigr\}, \label{eq:conference-objectives}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_A$ and $\Delta_R$ denote the aggregate and rare-risk differences.
The cross moment is the sum of the product of the coefficient biases and the
covariance of their errors.

\begin{proposition}[Objective disagreement under fixed calibration]
  \label{thm:conference-c2}
  Take $q = .02$, $\ell \in[.9,1]$, $\beta^* = (2,1)^\top$, $h = .01$, $B = 1$,
  $k = M = 100$, and $\sigma^2 \in[3.8,4.1]$. Both U and S are mean-square
  stable, and uniformly over these intervals,
  \begin{align*}
    .00077 & \leq R_k^S - R_k^U \leq .00194,         \\
    -.0983 & \leq R_{R,k}^S - R_{R,k}^U \leq -.0233.
  \end{align*}
  Moreover, $V_{CC,k}^S > V_{CC,k}^U$ and $V_{RR,k}^S > V_{RR,k}^U$.
\end{proposition}

SD scaling increases each coefficient's mean squared error but improves
rare-presence prediction. At $\ell = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 4$,
\[
  \Delta V \approx \begin{pmatrix}
                     .022743  & -.615146 \\
                     -.615146 & .470335
                   \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The reduction in the cross moment offsets the larger individual errors when
predicting for the rare subgroup. Aggregate risk gives this moment weight $.01$,
whereas rare risk gives it weight $.49$. Looking only at the individual
coefficient errors would therefore miss the improvement in rare prediction.

We prove both comparisons using rational interval arithmetic with outward
rounding over the full parameter boxes. \Cref{sec:comparison-proofs} gives the
recurrences and enclosure arguments. The results apply to the specified training
procedures, whereas \cref{thm:conference-predictive} compares risks after
optimizing over all constant rates and deterministic stops. Adaptive stopping
and the choice of an optimal normalization remain outside these results. The
identity relating the two risk objectives is elementary.

\subsection{Proofs and certificates}
\label{sec:comparison-proofs}

\citet[Section 3.2]{wadia2021whitening} explain how whitening changes
early-stopped predictions in full-rank least squares despite leaving the unique
optimum unchanged. Their gradient-flow calculation shows that whitening removes
differences in learning speed across covariance eigendirections, which can cause
signal and noise to be learned together. This is a precedent for the mechanism
illustrated in \cref{fig:intro-coefficient-paths}. Our theorem compares total
rare-subgroup risk for final iterates of fresh-sample SGD, with each scaling
optimizing its constant rate and deterministic stop under a finite observation
cap.

The closest preconditioning results differ in their risk objectives and training
procedures. \citet[Proposition 7]{amari2020preconditioning} show that signal
alignment affects the preferred preconditioner after optimizing aggregate
prediction bias over stopping times. They study gradient flow on a fixed
training sample in an overparameterized model, averaging over signal priors. For
fresh-sample SGD, \citet[Theorems 4.2--4.4]{su2024preconditioning} bound the
aggregate excess risk of tail-averaged iterates and tune preconditioning to
match ridge regression up to constant factors.

The target-risk literature also studies different comparisons.
\citet[Remark 3.1]{lei2021shift} give a minimax target-risk separation from
optimally tuned ridge with a fixed design and Gaussian noise.
\citet[Corollary 3.3]{wu2022pretraining} analyze target risk of the final
online-SGD iterate with geometric step decay, and \citet{liu2025precondition}
study minimax preconditioning and accelerated SGD under covariate shift. These
results do not supply our explicit opposite gaps for fixed signal families. Our
theorem compares total rare-subgroup risk of final iterates after optimizing all
nonnegative constant rates and deterministic stops under one observation cap.

Our calculations build on established mechanisms. Early stopping changes
least-squares regularization \citep{ali2019early}. \citet{ali2020stochastic}
compare stochastic gradient flow with ridge along the training path for a fixed
design. Their Lemma 1 matches discrete SGD's first two moments to an Euler
discretization of the flow; the continuous process is an approximation to SGD.
Analyses of least-mean-squares methods separate bias and variance while
accounting for sampling and batching \citep{defossez2015averaged,
jain2018parallelizing}. AdaBatch connects sparse mini-batch aggregation with
coordinate rescaling \citep{defossez2017adabatch}, while moment operators
characterize exact mean-square stability \citep{mulayoff2024exact}. For explicit
regularization, \citet{larsson2025normalization} analyze how feature scaling
affects shrinkage.

The risks of scaling rare features also have precedents.
\citet[Section 5.4]{bordes2010erratum} warn that standardizing very rare
features can hurt test performance. In a study of deterministic graph neural
network training, \citet[Section 5]{milligan2024normalization} identify
increased stochastic variance as a possible cost of standardizing rare binary
features. These observations motivate quantitative risk comparisons but do not
establish our oracle inequalities.

In this appendix, we derive the moment recurrences and approximation bounds used
to prove \cref{thm:conference-c1,thm:conference-c2}. We then describe the
interval certificates. Throughout, we use the binary model, fresh sampling, and
population preprocessing of \cref{sec:conference-setting}, allowing the
dependence introduced in \cref{sec:conference-comparisons}. The implementation
is in \path{checks/normalization_risk_theorem.py}. The source supplement
includes this implementation, which checks both the certificates and independent
numerical calculations; its \texttt{README.md} gives reproduction instructions.

\subsubsection{Exact moments and the variance decomposition}

Write $D = \eta P = \diag(d_C,d_R)$ and
$s_k = (V_{CC,k},V_{CR,k},V_{RR,k})^\top$. Set
\[
  t_B = c^2 + \frac{ab - c^2}{B},\qquad m_B
      = c\left( b + \frac{1 - 4b}{B} \right).
\]
The exact recurrence is $s_{k+1} = L_Ds_k + \sigma^2g_D$, where
\[
  L_D
  = \begin{pmatrix}
      (1 - d_Ca)^2    & -2d_C(1 - d_Ca)c                 & d_C^2t_B                        \\
      -d_R(1 - d_Ca)c & (1 - d_Ca)(1 - d_Rb) + d_Cd_Rt_B & -d_Cc + d_Cd_Rm_B               \\
      d_R^2t_B        & -2d_Rc + 2d_R^2m_B               & (1 - d_Rb)^2 + d_R^2b(1 - 4b)/B
    \end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
  g_D = \frac1B(d_C^2a,d_Cd_Rc,d_R^2b)^\top,\qquad s_0
      = ((\beta_C^*)^2,\beta_C^*\beta_R^*,(\beta_R^*)^2)^\top.
\]
To verify the recurrence, let $\widehat C$ be the average second moment in a
batch. The squared common coordinate is always $a$, and the binary law gives
\[
  \E(x_R^4) = b(1 - 3b),\quad \E(x_C^2x_R^2)
            = ab,\quad \E(x_Cx_R^3)
            = c(1 - 3b).
\]
Consequently, $\E(\widehat c^2) = t_B$, $\E(\widehat c\widehat b) = m_B$, and
$\E(\widehat b^2) = b^2 + b(1 - 4b)/B$. Expanding
$\E\bigl((I - D\widehat C)V_k(I - \widehat CD)\bigr)$ yields $L_D$. The
conditional mean-zero noise assumption removes the cross terms, and the added
noise has covariance $\sigma^2DCD/B$. Because each batch is fresh, its update
matrix is independent of the previous error.

To prove \cref{eq:conference-decomposition}, we run the noiseless recursion on
the same feature stream. Its mean error is $e_k = -(I - DC)^k\beta^*$ and its
covariance is $A_k \succeq 0$. Conditional on all sampled features, the
component due to noise has mean zero, so its cross moment with the noiseless
component vanishes. Its covariance is $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$, which proves the
decomposition. Equivalently, if
$\mathcal T_D(V) = \E\bigl((I - D\widehat C)V(I - \widehat CD)\bigr)$, then
\[
  V_k = \mathcal T_D^k(\beta^*\beta^{*\top})
        + \frac{\sigma^2}{B}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal T_D^j(DCD).
\]

\subsubsection{A scalar approximation with a controlled remainder}

Under independence, the diagonal moments satisfy a closed recurrence with matrix
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    K_D = \begin{pmatrix}
            A & e   \\
            f & D_0
          \end{pmatrix},\qquad A = (1 - h)^2, \\
    e = \frac{d_C^2ab}{B},\qquad f = \frac{d_R^2ab}{B},\qquad D_0 = (1 - d_Rb)^2 + \frac{d_R^2b(1 - 4b)}B.
  \end{gathered}
\]
Here $D_0$ is a scalar, distinct from $D$. All entries are nonnegative. Define
\[
  w = \tfrac12(a,(1 - q)^2)^\top,\quad v_0
    = ((\beta_C^*)^2,(\beta_R^*)^2)^\top,\quad g
    = (d_C^2a,d_R^2b)^\top/B,
\]
and let $E_0 = \max(e,f)$ and $\|K_D\|_\infty \leq \rho < 1$. For
$\phi_k(z) = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}z^j$, define
\[
  \widetilde R_{R,k} = w_C\{A^k(\beta_C^*)^2 + \sigma^2g_C\phi_k(A)\}
                       + w_R\{D_0^k(\beta_R^*)^2 + \sigma^2g_R\phi_k(D_0)\}.
\]
For every $k \geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
  0
  \leq R_{R,k} - \widetilde R_{R,k}
  \leq E_F + \sigma^2E_N,\qquad E_F
  = \|w\|_1\|v_0\|_\infty E_0k\rho^{k-1},\quad E_N
  = \frac{\|w\|_1\|g\|_\infty E_0}{(1 - \rho)^2}. \label{eq:comparison-remainder}
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $K_0 = \diag(A,D_0)$ and $E = K_D - K_0 \geq 0$ entrywise,
\[
  K_D^k - K_0^k = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}K_D^{k-1-j}EK_0^j \geq 0.
\]
The difference has infinity norm at most $E_0k\rho^{k-1}$. Multiplying by $w$
and $v_0$ proves the homogeneous bound. We obtain the noise bound by applying
the same identity to the noise sum and using
$\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}j\rho^{j-1} \leq (1 - \rho)^{-2}$.

The common-coordinate terms cancel when we subtract the U approximation from the
S approximation. Write
\[
  \widetilde F = w_R(\beta_R^*)^2(D_S^k - D_U^k),\qquad \widetilde N
               = w_R\{g_{R,S}\phi_k(D_S) - g_{R,U}\phi_k(D_U)\},
\]
where $D_S,D_U$ are the respective scalar $D_0$ values. For the exact difference
$F + \sigma^2N$, subtracting the two nonnegative remainders gives
\[
  F_- := \widetilde F - E_{F,U} \leq F \leq \widetilde F + E_{F,S} = :F_+,
\]
\[
  N_- := \widetilde N - E_{N,U} \leq N \leq \widetilde N + E_{N,S} = :N_+.
\]
When $F_+ < 0 < N_-$, division by positive denominators gives
\cref{eq:conference-threshold}. In particular, $F_+ + \sigma^2N_+ < 0$
guarantees lower rare risk for S, whereas $F_- + \sigma^2N_- > 0$ guarantees
lower rare risk for U. The homogeneous approximation includes variation from
rare-feature sampling through $D_0$, in addition to the squared mean error.

\subsubsection{
  Certificate for
  \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:conference-c1}}{Theorem~\ref*{thm:conference-c1}}
}

We partition each of the $q$ and $h$ intervals into eight equal closed pieces.
On each of the resulting 64 rectangles, we evaluate the preceding bounds using
interval arithmetic and $(\beta_C^*)^2,(\beta_R^*)^2 \in[.9025,1.1025]$. For
each configuration, we use upper bounds on $\rho = \max(A + e,D_0 + f)$ and
$E_0 = \max(e,f)$. This gives $\rho < .999719$ throughout. The remaining cross
moment is multiplied at each step by
\[
  \gamma = (1 - d_Ca)(1 - d_Rb) + d_Cd_Rab/B
\]
with no added noise term. The certificate verifies $0 \leq \gamma < 1$ on every
rectangle. The full homogeneous second-moment map is therefore stable, and the
moments remain bounded when we include the fixed finite noise term.

Every rectangle satisfies $F_+ < 0 < N_-$. Taking the hull of the resulting
$F + \sigma^2N$ intervals gives the bounds in \cref{tab:conference-c1}. In table
order, the enclosures for the noise threshold are $[10.46,17.76]$,
$[.53,1.405]$, $[112.61,184.22]$, and $[5.69,14.49]$. They imply the four signs
uniformly whenever $1.405 < \sigma^2 < 5.69$. The narrower interval in the
theorem gives the displayed risk-gap bounds. Because the rectangles are closed,
the certificate also covers their shared boundaries.

\subsubsection{Outward-rounded arithmetic}

We represent each interval endpoint as an integer multiple of $10^{-60}$ and
read inputs as rational strings or integers. Addition is exact. For
multiplication, we take the minimum and maximum of all four endpoint products
and round outward using integer division. If $[l,u]$ stores the integer
endpoints of a positive denominator interval, we enclose its reciprocal by
\[
  \left[ \frac{\lfloor10^{120}/u\rfloor}{10^{60}}, \frac{\lceil10^{120}/l\rceil}{10^{60}} \right].
\]
We reduce negative denominators to this case and compute powers and finite sums
by repeated squaring and addition. Induction over these operations shows that
each interval contains the exact rational expression. All acceptance checks
compare integer endpoints, including the check that they lie within the reported
decimal bounds. We convert to floating point only for display. The enclosure
calculations therefore cover the full parameter boxes.

\subsubsection{
  Proof of
  \texorpdfstring{\Cref{thm:conference-c2}}{Theorem~\ref*{thm:conference-c2}}
}

To establish stability, we use the fact that a feature vector with
$\|z\|^2 \leq L$ satisfies
\[
  \E\bigl((I - \eta zz^\top)^2\bigr) \preceq I - \eta(2 - \eta L)H, \qquad H
                                     = \E(zz^\top).
\]
If $H \succ 0$ and $0 < \eta < 2/L$, this contracts expected squared error. For
U, take $L = .25 + .98^2 = 1.2104$, $\eta = .04$, and use
$\lambda_{\min}(C) \geq .0196 - .01 = .0096$. For S, $L = 50$, $\eta = .01$, and
\[
  H = \begin{pmatrix}
        1      & \ell/7 \\
        \ell/7 & 1
      \end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_{\min}(H)
    \geq 6/7.
\]
These sufficient stability bounds hold throughout the claimed interval.

To bound the risks, we augment $L_D$ with the unit-noise column $g_D$ and a
final row $(0,0,0,1)$. Raising this matrix to the $100$th power gives the
homogeneous map and finite noise sum. The aggregate and rare risk weights on
$s_k$ are
\[
  w_A = (a/2,c,b/2)^\top,\qquad w_R = (a/2,\ell(1 - q)/2,(1 - q)^2/2)^\top.
\]
We partition $[.9,1]$ into 128 equal closed intervals. On each interval, we use
the same integer arithmetic to enclose the two augmented powers, their action on
$s_0 = (4,2,1)^\top$, and the contrasts $F_W + \sigma^2N_W$ for
$\sigma^2 \in[3.8,4.1]$. Signed matrix entries use all four endpoint products;
no entrywise positivity is assumed. The union satisfies both risk enclosures in
the theorem. Before risk weighting, the same powers certify positive diagonal
contrasts and a negative cross-moment contrast throughout. This proves the
coefficient MSE inequalities as well.

\subsubsection{Checks distinct from the certificates}

We independently check the three-moment formulas against gradient updates by
enumerating ordered batches and short paths. We also compare them with a
separate full moment operator at 64 endpoints, including signals with mixed
signs, boundaries of the dependence range, an interior law, and $B = 1,2$. These
floating-point checks validate the implementations. The interval arithmetic
above certifies the inequalities over the continuous parameter boxes. Neither
check establishes priority or extends the results to procedures outside the
stated comparisons.

\section{Empirical methods}
\label{sec:empirical-methods}

The controlled study isolates changes to the oracle training model and compares
calibration with adaptive selection. The sampled binary and Mushroom studies ask
how validation coverage, the candidate search, and preprocessing affect
practical comparisons. All comparisons pair the scaling rules within each
replication or partition. The protocols below specify which data and candidates
each comparison uses.

\subsection{Controlled departures from the oracle model}
\label{sec:theory-bridge-protocol}

The study in \cref{sec:theory-bridge} uses
$I \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(1/2)$ and $J \sim \operatorname{Bernoulli}(q)$
independently, with $q \in \{.02,.005\}$. Responses are
$Y = \beta_C^*(I - 1/2) + \beta_R^*(J - q) + \varepsilon$, where
$\varepsilon \sim N(0,1)$ is independent and the coefficient pairs are $(.1,1)$
and $(1,.1)$. Each update processes one observation. Caps are $(52,102,204)$ at
$q = .02$ and $(202,402,804)$ at $q = .005$, all satisfying
$1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$. Settings were specified before examining the study's
outcomes; this is an exploratory extension of the existing theorem.

\subsubsection{Training arms and pairing}

\Cref{tab:bridge-arms} specifies the eight arms. The first 256 rows of an
independent feature and noise stream form the fixed training pool. Fresh SGD
uses successive rows of that stream; reuse samples pool indices independently
with replacement, retaining each row's original noise. Common random numbers
pair the two scalings, signal pairs, training arms, and candidate paths within
each replication. Estimated preprocessing uses the entire pool, including rows
beyond the SGD cap when $M < 256$. These arms therefore use additional data for
preprocessing, beyond the observations counted by the update cap.

\subsubsection{Candidate family and calibration}

Let $z(i,j)$ denote the transformed feature vector under a given arm and
scaling, augmented by a unit coordinate when fitting an intercept. Define
\[
  L = \max_{(i,j) \in \{0,1\}^2}\|z(i,j)\|^2, \qquad \eta = t/L.
\]
The rate factors are zero and 129 geometric values from $10^{-4}$ to $1.9$. Each
homogeneous update $I - \eta zz^\top$ is nonexpansive because
$0 \leq \eta\|z\|^2 \leq 1.9 < 2$. This condition rules out expansion by an
individual homogeneous update; it does not bound accumulated response noise or
guarantee good predictions on an unseen subgroup. It is a conservative search
restriction, not a characterization of every stable constant rate. For estimated
preprocessing, $L$ depends on the training pool. Calibration fixes $t$, while
each resulting trajectory retains one constant $\eta$.

Stopping checkpoints are the distinct integers obtained by rounding 65 geometric
points from one to the largest cap, together with zero and all three caps. A
nested coarse grid retains every second positive rate factor and every second
checkpoint, always including zero and the caps. Both scalings receive the same
number of trajectories. At cap $M$, nominal search cost is $130M$ processed
observations per arm, scaling, signal, and replication; caps and selection
policies share paths. Preprocessing, validation scoring, and calibration require
additional resources.

For each prevalence, 2048 calibration replications determine the mean exact
population rare risk of every candidate. Each arm, scaling, signal, and cap then
selects a rate factor and eligible stop. The fresh, fixed-intercept arm instead
uses exact expected moments for this choice. An independent set of 4096
evaluation replications assesses the frozen choices. The two stages use distinct
random streams derived from seed 20260924. This procedure estimates a
finite-family optimum conditional on the calibration run, rather than the
unrestricted oracle in \cref{thm:conference-predictive}.

\subsubsection{Effects of changing training}

Both orderings persist in all 96 comparisons: SD has lower rare risk for the
dominant rare signal, and U has lower rare risk for the dominant common signal
(\cref{fig:bridge-controlled}). Each gap exceeds nine paired Monte Carlo
standard errors in magnitude, conditional on the calibration choices. A coarser
rate and stopping grid preserves all 96 signs. These results concern the two
tested signal pairs and do not extend the theorem to the changed training
procedures.

\subsubsection{Adaptive selection and exact evaluation}

Each evaluation replication also supplies an independent validation dataset with
2048 rows. Nested prefixes of sizes 128, 512, and 2048 select candidates by rare
validation half squared loss, using aggregate loss if the rare group is empty.
Ties favor an earlier stop, then a smaller rate factor. Empty rare validation
groups occur in $(306,1,0)$ replications at $q = .02$ and $(2206,326,0)$ at
$q = .005$, in increasing sample-size order. The validation selectors never
observe population risk.

A further benchmark selects each replication's realized predictor with lowest
exact population rare risk. To distinguish the two objectives, let
$r_R(\widehat f_{D,a})$ be the population rare excess risk of candidate $a$
trained on random data and sampling choices $D$. For a fixed finite candidate
index set $\mathcal A$, including the allowed rate factors and stops,
\[
  \E_D\!\left[ \min_{a \in \mathcal A}r_R(\widehat f_{D,a}) \right] \leq \min_{a \in \mathcal A}\E_D\!\left[ r_R(\widehat f_{D,a}) \right].
\]

The left side allows adaptation to realized training randomness. The calibrated
procedure approximates the right side, with its choices learned on separate
replications. Neither side here is an unrestricted rate search, and a lower left
side for each scaling does not preserve their ordering. The reversal under exact
selection in \cref{tab:bridge-selection} therefore concerns a different
objective from the theorem, even without validation noise.

\Cref{fig:bridge-validation} shows how validation selection changes the
comparisons across both prevalences and signals. Missing validation observations
create a further problem. For the fresh baseline with $q = .005$, $M = 804$, and
a dominant rare signal, validation with 128 rows changes the gap from $-.0216$
to $+1.133$ (standard error $.062$). The rare group is absent in 2206 of 4096
validation sets. Conditional on its absence, the gap is $+2.193$; when it is
present, the gap is $-.105$. Even within this conservative rate search,
validation can miss the group on which the two scalings differ most.

Evaluation enumerates the four binary cells analytically and excludes
irreducible test noise. For a predictor written in population-centered
coordinates as $a + b_C(I - 1/2) + b_R(J - q)$, its rare excess risk is
\[
  r_R = \frac{(b_C - \beta_C^*)^2}{8}
        + \frac{\{a + (1 - q)(b_R - \beta_R^*)\}^2}{2}.
\]
This includes the fitted intercept and any shift induced by estimated centering.
No prediction clipping is applied. We average paired SD-minus-U differences
across evaluation replications and report their Monte Carlo standard errors.
These errors condition on the frozen calibration choices; they exclude
calibration uncertainty and adjustments for multiple comparisons.

\subsubsection{Mushroom data and partitions}

We fit a squared-loss linear predictor to the recorded poisonous label in the
UCI Mushroom dataset \citep{uciMushroom}, using all 22 categorical predictors.
The declared subgroup consists of mushrooms with green spore-print color, which
accounts for 72 of 8124 records. The records are hypothetical descriptions
derived from a field guide, so evaluation on random partitions does not
establish generalization to new species or field data. There are no duplicate
predictor rows. We construct the vocabulary and estimate centering and scales
from training data alone, treat missingness as a category, and map unknown
categories to zero blocks. We store the one-hot design sparsely and implement
centering algebraically. Because the full one-hot design is rank deficient, its
coefficients depend on the optimizer and cannot be interpreted as uniquely
identified effects.

We use 32 random partitions for each pair of training and validation sizes,
$(256,128)$ and $(2048,1024)$, holding out the remaining records for testing.
All candidates share partitions and replacement index streams. We use batches
$B \in \{16,128\}$ and caps $M \in \{512,2048,8192\}$, and do not clip
predictions.

\subsection{Candidate searches and validation rules}
\label{sec:practical-searches}

The main Mushroom comparison uses four rules: no scaling, SD, damping with
$\lambda = .01$, and variance scaling, with divisors $1$, $\sqrt{v_j}$,
$\sqrt{v_j + .01}$, and $v_j$, respectively. Population variances are used only
in the synthetic arm with known preprocessing; all other arms use training
variances with denominator $n$. Each rule receives the same 25-rate search. The
five-rate reference compares no scaling, SD, and damping. The 17-rate Mushroom
search compares these three rules across preprocessing arms; the separate
damping comparison adds variance scaling and four other fixed offsets in the
centered arm with a fitted intercept.

All these searches set $\eta = a/\max(L,10^{-12})$, where $L$ is the largest
empirical curvature, including the unscaled intercept coordinate when fitted.
This calibration does not guarantee stochastic stability. The five-rate grid is
\[

The 17-rate grid contains
\[

\subsection{Fixed damping offsets}
\label{sec:mushroom-sensitivity}

Damping limits the amplification of low-variance features, since
$P_{jj} \leq 1/\lambda$. At the same learning rate, it multiplies the SD update
on the original coefficient scale by $v_j/(v_j + \lambda)$. Thus $\lambda$ is
the feature variance at which damping halves the SD update; features with much
larger variance remain close to SD scaling. We fix the offset in variance units
before evaluation, without deriving it from theory or selecting it by
validation.

For diagonal $C$, the population rule $P_\lambda = (C + \lambda I)^{-1}$
satisfies $\lambda P_\lambda = (\lambda^{-1}C + I)^{-1}$. It therefore belongs,
up to a scalar absorbed into the learning rate, to the preconditioner family of
\citet{su2024preconditioning}.

To examine sensitivity to the fixed heuristic offset $0.01$ in
\cref{eq:conference-damped}, we compare five offsets
$\lambda \in \{0.001,0.003,0.01,0.03,0.1\}$ for $s_j = \sqrt{v_j + \lambda}$,
together with no scaling, SD ($\lambda = 0$), and variance scaling
($s_j = v_j$). We reuse both sample sizes and all 32 partitions per size, and
restrict the comparison to centered predictors with a fitted intercept. Each
fixed rule receives the same 17 rate factors, two batch sizes, and stopping
searches from \cref{sec:practical-searches}. Comparing the five positive offsets
therefore requires five times the work of evaluating one fixed rule. We do not
select an offset by test loss.

\subsection{Validation coverage and high-precision replay}
\label{sec:five-rate-replay}

In the five-rate synthetic search, the two laws with $q = 0.002$ have no rare
validation observations in 106 and 105 of 128 replications. Validation can then
select a finite but explosive rare coefficient because no rare cases are
available to reveal its error. One SD fit with centered predictors has a rare
coefficient of about $-6.719 \times 10^{16}$ and validation half squared loss
$2.086$, yet its exact rare loss over the binary cells is about
$2.257 \times 10^{33}$. We retain this path in the results. In a separate
investigation, we replay every eligible trajectory for the worst selected
replication in each of 24 combinations of law and scaling, using 160-digit
decimal arithmetic. All 24 selections remain unchanged, so the detected
floating-point cancellation does not explain the failure. Means dominated by
these extreme finite outcomes do not precisely estimate population risk. We also
retain all 151 failed synthetic endpoints, flagged in the archive.

\subsection{Replay discrepancies in the 25-rate search}

The audit reconstructs every loss and selection and replays 100,271 paths in
explicitly transformed coordinates. Coefficient paths shared with the five- and
17-rate searches reproduce exactly. Reconstructed losses have maximum normwise
discrepancy $1.36 \times 10^{-14}$ and leave every selection unchanged. Mushroom
passes the declared trajectory and prediction tolerances. For synthetic data,
however, the audit retains 63 discrepancies in twelve replications, all of which
have empty rare validation groups. These comprise 60 checks of selected
predictions, two first-failure times, and one trajectory check. Repeated queries
can refer to the same selected path. The maximum normwise coefficient and
prediction discrepancies are $.003747$ and $.004184$, exceeding the declared
tolerances of $2 \times 10^{-9}$ and $10^{-8}$. All stored checkpoint statuses
agree.

The transformed replay therefore does not certify these extreme synthetic paths.
Flagged selections dominate the rarer C2 mean in
\cref{sec:variance-scaling-followup}, so its magnitude and its ranking against
other scaling rules remain unverified. The records below map all retained
discrepancies to their queries and affected summaries. We retain all candidates
and selections in the archive. The audit records the affected configurations and
disagreements in failure times. The settings are in
\path{experiments/common/rates25.py}; saved outputs are in

\subsection{Clipping without changing selection}

For $y \in[0,1]$, projection of any prediction onto $[0,1]$ satisfies
\[
  \bigl(\Pi_{[0,1]}(\widehat y) - y\bigr)^2 \leq (\widehat y - y)^2.
\]
We apply this transformation to the saved predictors selected by the 25-factor
search, with standard stopping checkpoints, cap $8192$, training centering, and
a fitted intercept. Both sample sizes retain all 32 partitions and both
validation objectives. We reconstruct test predictions from the saved
original-scale coefficients and the training-derived vocabulary, check their raw
losses against the archive, and verify the clipping inequality for every test
observation. This changes neither training nor validation selection. In
particular, we do not rescore candidates using clipped validation predictions.

\section{Source supplement}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

The source supplement accompanies the manuscript and contains the paper source,
experiment and plotting code, source data, saved results, numerical checks, and
pinned environment definitions. Its \texttt{README.md} gives the setup,
container, and reproduction instructions, including how to build the complete
paper from saved results, rerun experiments, and check the numerical
certificates. It also identifies optional outputs that must be regenerated.

The certificate checkers are \path{checks/predictive_fixed_q.py} for
\cref{thm:conference-predictive} and \path{checks/normalization_risk_theorem.py}
for \cref{thm:conference-c1,thm:conference-c2}. Their recorded standard output
is in \path{results/certificates/predictive-fixed-q.txt} and
\path{results/certificates/normalization-risk.txt}. From the extracted archive,
enter \texttt{devenv shell} and run
\begin{verbatim}
task check:predictive-fixed-q
task check:normalization-risk
\end{verbatim}
These commands recompute the certificates without training models. The first
checks the six continuous parameter regions in \cref{sec:predictive-proofs},
including exact rational bounds and coverage volumes; the second checks the
supporting fixed-calibration comparisons. The recorded output is a summary of
the completed checks, not a substitute for rerunning the checkers.

We ran the experiments on a local NixOS workstation with an AMD Ryzen 9 7900 CPU
(12 cores, 24 hardware threads) and approximately 62\,GiB of usable RAM. The
Python implementations use NumPy and SciPy on the CPU, with OpenBLAS restricted
to one thread by the experiment tasks. Saved experiment metadata include
software versions and elapsed runtimes.
