\begin{abstract}
  Feature normalization changes how quickly stochastic gradient descent learns
  different coefficients. We study how this affects prediction under a finite
  training budget, using common and rare binary features. We derive exact error
  moments that separate incomplete learning from variation due to feature
  sampling and observation noise. For two independent features with known
  population preprocessing and one fresh observation per update, we show that
  standardization gives lower rare-subgroup risk on one signal family, whereas
  unscaled training gives lower risk on another. Each method may choose any
  nonnegative constant learning rate and any deterministic stop within the same
  budget. The risk gaps remain bounded away from zero as prevalence decreases,
  provided that the expected number of rare observations stays between roughly
  one and four. Thus tuning the learning rate and stopping time need not remove
  the effect of relative feature scaling. Further comparisons show how stopping,
  batching, and dependence affect risk under a fixed calibration, although
  retuning removes both original anchor comparisons. We also study estimated
  preprocessing and validation selection. On a sparse categorical task,
  expanding the rate search removes the resolved advantage of standardization
  under aggregate selection, while damped scaling retains lower risks. Missing
  rare validation cases can conceal severe subgroup errors. These experiments
  motivated the theory, whose predictions still require prospective evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Feature normalization is often motivated by placing predictors on a common
scale. With binary and continuous predictors, a standard-deviation change
measures population variation, whereas a binary change from absence to presence
defines a specific contrast. These conventions can imply different
regularization strengths. We study binary predictors to isolate prevalence: even
with a shared absence-to-presence coding, standardization changes how strongly
training responds to their effects.

A rare feature gives a model few opportunities to learn its effect. Without
scaling, gradient descent may fit common features while leaving the rare feature
largely unlearned. Dividing by the feature's standard deviation increases the
influence of those few observations. This can help recover a useful rare signal,
but it also makes the fitted coefficient more sensitive to noise.

These effects can substantially change prediction where the rare feature is
present, despite contributing little to average error. Normalization therefore
raises a statistical question: how strongly should training respond to scarce
evidence? Scaling leaves the full-rank least-squares solution unchanged but
changes which signals we learn before stopping.

Learning-rate and stopping-time tuning need not remove this effect.
Figure~\ref{fig:conference-tuned-intro} illustrates the central comparison:
standardization gives lower rare-subgroup risk when the rare signal dominates,
whereas unscaled training gives lower risk when the common signal dominates.
Each rule selects its own rate and stop under the same observation cap. Learning
rare signals has a noise cost, and normalization changes that tradeoff.

Our main theorem establishes both orderings for two independent binary
predictors under squared loss, with known population centering and scales, zero
initialization, a fixed zero intercept, and one fresh observation per update. It
covers all nonnegative constant rates and deterministic stops within the budget.
The gaps stay bounded away from zero as prevalence decreases while the expected
number of rare observations stays between roughly one and four. Learning-rate
schedules and data-dependent selection remain outside its scope.

Under a fixed rate calibration, supporting comparisons show that the preferred
normalization can change with stopping, batching, and the prediction objective.
Tuning removes both original anchor comparisons, motivating the oracle theorem.
Experiments with estimated preprocessing and validation selection show that
missing rare validation cases can conceal extreme subgroup errors. On a sparse
categorical task, expanding the rate search removes the resolved SD advantage
under aggregate selection, while damping retains lower risks. These experiments
informed the theorem; prospective evaluation remains open.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}

Several authors have studied how preconditioning affects statistical performance
\citep{amari2020preconditioning, su2024preconditioning}. In particular,
\citet[Section 4.2, Proposition 7]{amari2020preconditioning} show that the
preferred preconditioner depends on signal alignment even after optimizing
early-stopping bias in an overparameterized model with fixed data. We instead
compare total rare-subgroup risk, including variance from stochastic updates, in
a fixed-dimensional model with fresh observations. Under the same observation
cap, we obtain explicit gaps between the best risks attainable over all constant
rates and deterministic stops.

Early stopping also changes least-squares regularization \citep{ali2019early},
and analyses of least-mean-squares methods separate bias and variance while
accounting for sampling and batching \citep{defossez2015averaged,
jain2018parallelizing}. AdaBatch connects sparse mini-batch aggregation with
coordinate rescaling \citep{defossez2017adabatch}, while moment operators
characterize exact mean-square stability \citep{mulayoff2024exact}.
\citet{liu2025precondition} study how the test objective affects preconditioning
under covariate shift. For explicit regularization,
\citet{larsson2025normalization} analyze how feature scaling affects shrinkage.
We build on these mechanisms to compare conventional normalization rules using
final iterates, coefficients on the original scale, and rare-presence risk. Our
contribution is the quantitative comparison and its dependence on tuning;
diagonal preconditioning and the bias--variance tradeoff are established
mechanisms.

\section{Normalization and the training procedure}
\label{sec:conference-setting}

Let $x$ be a centered feature vector with $C = \E[xx^\top] \succ 0$, and suppose
that $y = x^\top\beta^* + \varepsilon$, where $\E[\varepsilon\mid x] = 0$ and
$\E[\varepsilon^2\mid x] = \sigma^2$. We divide each feature by a positive scale
factor, collected in the diagonal matrix $S$. Training on $z = S^{-1}x$ with
parameter $\theta = S\beta$ then gives the following SGD update on the original
coefficient scale:
\begin{equation}
  \beta_{k+1}
  = \beta_k
    - \frac{\eta S^{-2}}{B} \sum_{i=1}^B x_{k,i}(x_{k,i}^\top\beta_k - y_{k,i}). \label{eq:conference-update}
\end{equation}
We use the average squared loss with a factor $1/2$. In the population
comparisons, we fix the centered intercept at zero and initialize $\beta_0 = 0$.
Each update uses $B$ independent fresh observations, so $k$ updates require
$M = kB$ observations. In Section~\ref{sec:conference-experiments}, we also
sample with replacement from a fixed training set of size $n$. There, $M$ counts
processed observations and can exceed $n$.

We can compensate exactly for global rescaling by adjusting the learning rate.
Replacing $S$ by $gS$ and $\eta$ by $g^2\eta$ leaves every update unchanged when
the methods use the same data stream. Changing the relative feature scales,
however, generally changes $\eta S^{-2}$ in a way that a single learning rate
cannot undo. Whether this leads to a risk difference after tuning requires a
separate comparison.

Write $e_k = \E[\beta_k-\beta^*]$ and
$V_k = \E[(\beta_k-\beta^*)(\beta_k-\beta^*)^\top]$. Fresh sampling gives
\begin{equation}
  V_k = e_ke_k^\top + A_k + \sigma^2N_k, \label{eq:conference-decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $A_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance of the noiseless process under random
feature sampling and $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$ is the covariance contributed by
observation noise. This decomposition separates incomplete learning from the two
sources of variation in training. We derive the exact recurrence in
Appendix~\ref{sec:comparison-proofs}.

Under independence, $e_{k+1,j} = (1 - \eta C_{jj}/s_j^2)e_{k,j}$. SD scaling,
with $s_j^2 = C_{jj}$, gives every coordinate the multiplier $1 - \eta$. For
$0 < \eta \leq 1$ and zero initialization, early stopping thus gives every
coefficient the same shrinkage factor in expectation. This equalizes one aspect
of implicit regularization, but coefficient uncertainties also depend on the
sampling and noise covariances in \eqref{eq:conference-decomposition}.

For a subgroup $G$, we define the aggregate and subgroup excess prediction risks
using half squared loss:
\begin{equation}
  R_k = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(CV_k),\qquad R_{G,k}
      = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(C_GV_k), \label{eq:conference-risks}
\end{equation}
Here $C_G = \E[xx^\top\mid G]$ is the conditional second moment, which includes
the contribution of the conditional feature mean. Both risks exclude irreducible
test noise. We evaluate both objectives for the same trained predictor, without
changing the distribution of its training data.

\paragraph{Binary features.}

Let $I$ and $J$ be common and rare binary features, respectively, with
\[
  \begin{gathered}
    \Prob(I = 1) = \tfrac12,\qquad \Prob(J = 1) = q, \\
    \Prob(I = 1,J = 1) = \tfrac{q(1+\ell)}2,
  \end{gathered}
\]
where $0 < q < 1/2$ and $-1 \leq \ell \leq 1$. These ranges ensure that all four
cell probabilities are nonnegative. For $x = (I - 1/2,J - q)^\top$, write
$a = 1/4$, $b = q(1 - q)$, and $c = q\ell/2$. Then
\[
  C = \begin{pmatrix}
        a & c \\
        c & b
      \end{pmatrix},\qquad C_R
    = \begin{pmatrix}
        a             & \ell(1 - q)/2 \\
        \ell(1 - q)/2 & (1 - q)^2
      \end{pmatrix},
\]
where $R$ denotes the subgroup $J = 1$. A rare-coordinate error of one
contributes $(1 - q)^2/2$ to rare risk and $q(1 - q)/2$ to aggregate risk.

We compare unscaled features (U), with $P_U = I_2$, and standard-deviation
scaling (S), with $P_S = \diag(a^{-1},b^{-1})$, where $P = S^{-2}$. In the main
theorem, each rule chooses its own constant learning rate and stopping count.
For the supporting comparisons, we calibrate the rates through the common
coordinate. We use the final iterate in both settings.

\paragraph{Damped scaling.}

The sampled-data experiments also include damped scaling, with
\begin{equation}
  s_j = \sqrt{v_j + 0.01},\qquad P_{jj}
      = \frac{1}{v_j + 0.01}, \label{eq:conference-damped}
\end{equation}
where $v_j$ is either the known population variance or an estimate from the
training data. We use the heuristic offset $0.01$ from the controlled pilot,
fixed in variance units before evaluation. We neither derive this value from
theory nor select it by validation. At the same learning rate, damping
multiplies the SD update on the original coefficient scale by
$v_j/(v_j + 0.01)$. This is approximately one half at binary prevalence $0.01$
and $0.96$ for a balanced binary feature, illustrating how the offset reduces
updates more strongly for rare features. The comparison does not establish an
optimal damping level.

\section{Opposite risk orderings after oracle tuning}
\label{sec:conference-predictive}

We now allow each normalization rule to choose its learning rate and stopping
count to minimize rare-presence risk. We assume independent features
($\ell = 0$), $B = 1$, and the population preprocessing, zero initialization,
and fixed intercept of Section~\ref{sec:conference-setting}. Put $b = q(1 - q)$.
We parameterize the update matrices on the original coefficient scale as
\begin{equation}
  D_U(\alpha) = \alpha I_2,\qquad D_S(\alpha)
              = \diag(4b\alpha,\alpha),\quad \alpha
              \geq 0. \label{eq:conference-oracle-rates}
\end{equation}
Here $D = \eta P$, so $\eta_U = \alpha$ and $\eta_S = b\alpha$. Each
parameterization covers every nonnegative constant learning rate for its rule.
We use the same parameter to describe both searches, but choose its value
separately for each method.

For an observation cap $M \in \mathbb N$, define
\begin{equation}
  R_A^*(M) = \inf_{\substack{\alpha\geq0\\ k\in\{0,\ldots,M\}}} R_{R,k}^{A}(\alpha),\qquad A
           \in \{U,S\}. \label{eq:conference-oracle-risk}
\end{equation}
The oracle chooses a deterministic rate and stop using population risk averaged
over training randomness. It may also choose initialization. Because we evaluate
finite stops, the search includes rates that need not be mean-square stable.

\begin{theorem}[Opposite oracle rare-risk orderings]
  \label{thm:conference-predictive}
  Suppose $\sigma^2 > 0$, $0 < q \leq 10^{-4}$, and $1 \leq Mq(1 - q) \leq 4$ in
  the independent binary model above.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item If $(\beta_R^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_C^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_U^*(M) - R_S^*(M) \geq 7\sigma^2/1000$.
    \item If $(\beta_C^*)^2 = \sigma^2$ and $(\beta_R^*)^2 \leq \sigma^2/100$,
          then $R_S^*(M) - R_U^*(M) \geq \sigma^2/200$.
  \end{enumerate}
  Either sign is allowed for each coefficient. Both inequalities also hold if
  each rule searches only its mean-square-stable nonnegative constant rates,
  together with initialization.
\end{theorem}

We prove the theorem in Appendix~\ref{sec:predictive-proofs} by bounding every
rate and stop for the method with higher risk and constructing one stable
candidate for the other method. The diagonal error moments satisfy an exact
positive recurrence, even though the stochastic updates couple the independent
features. When the rare signal dominates, learning it with U also introduces
noise in the common coefficient, and S can reduce this cost. When the common
signal dominates, S must balance incomplete learning of that signal against
noise in the rare coefficient. U can learn the common coefficient while leaving
the small rare signal largely unchanged. These bounds compare the best
attainable risks without identifying the minimizing rates and stops.

At the observation cap, the expected number of rare training observations is
$Mq = \tau/(1 - q)$, where $\tau = Mq(1 - q) \in[1,4]$. The positive gaps
therefore persist with roughly one to four rare observations, even as $M$ grows
and $q$ decreases. Which normalization has lower risk depends on the signal as
well as prevalence. The two families give sufficient conditions for opposite
orderings, but do not locate a general transition between them. The theorem
leaves batch selection, learning-rate schedules, and data-dependent stopping
open. It also provides no guarantee for estimated preprocessing or validation
selection.

\section{Supporting comparisons under a fixed calibration}
\label{sec:conference-comparisons}

In this section, we fix $\eta_U = 4h$ and $\eta_S = h$. Both rules then have
diagonal drift $h$ in the common coordinate. Under independence, this gives the
common coefficient the same mean trajectory for both rules, although dependence
generally breaks the match. We specify $h$ in advance and use it for both
methods.

\subsection{Learning the rare signal versus fitting noise}

Under independence, SD scaling changes the contraction factor for the mean rare
coefficient error from $1 - 4hb$ to $1 - h$. At the same time, it increases the
observation-noise variance added to that coefficient at each update from
$16h^2b\sigma^2/B$ to $h^2\sigma^2/(bB)$. Standardization therefore speeds up
learning while introducing more noise. Random feature sampling contributes
additional variance to both procedures.

The diagonal error moments satisfy a nonnegative two-dimensional recurrence. By
omitting the terms that couple the two coordinates, we obtain a scalar
approximation to rare risk with an explicit nonnegative remainder. Let $F$ and
$N$ denote the noiseless and unit-noise parts of the SD-minus-U risk difference.
The approximation gives bounds $F_- \leq F \leq F_+$ and $N_- \leq N \leq N_+$.
When $F_+ < 0 < N_-$, the noise-variance threshold $\tau = -F/N$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \frac{-F_+}{N_+} \leq \tau
                   \leq \frac{-F_-}{N_-}. \label{eq:conference-threshold}
\end{equation}
The remainder accounts for the dependence that the sampled updates introduce
between coefficient errors, even when the features are independent. We derive
the approximation and define its terms in Appendix~\ref{sec:comparison-proofs}.

\begin{theorem}[Stopping and batch reversal]
  \label{thm:conference-c1}
  In the binary model with $\ell = 0$, suppose
  \begin{gather*}
    q \in[0.019,0.021] ,\quad h \in[0.0019,0.0021] ,               \\
    \beta_C^*,\beta_R^* \in[0.95,1.05] ,\quad\sigma^2 \in[1.5,5] .
  \end{gather*}
  Both U and S are mean-square stable for $B \in \{1,10\}$. Their rare-risk
  contrast $\Delta_{B,k} = R_{R,k}^S - R_{R,k}^U$ lies in the intervals in
  Table~\ref{tab:conference-c1} throughout this parameter box.
\end{theorem}

At $B = 1$, the ordering reverses as training continues. With the same budget
$M = 10{,}000$, it also differs between $B = 1$ and $B = 10$. These are
comparisons between normalization rules at each batch size and budget. They do
not establish which batch size is better for either rule.

For the anchor $q = .02$, $h = .002$, $\beta^* = (1,1)^\top$, and
$\sigma^2 = 3$, the early $B = 1$ rare risk is $.356678$ for U and $.090407$ for
S. Squared bias contributes $.353207$ and $.011040$, respectively, so the early
advantage comes mainly from learning the rare signal more quickly. By
$k = 10{,}000$, U has learned enough of that signal to reach risk $.028145$,
whereas S has risk $.078788$, almost entirely due to observation noise. The
exact calculation includes variation from feature sampling at every step.

\subsection{Aggregate and rare-presence objectives}

Dependence can make the two prediction objectives favor different rules. Let
$\Delta V = V_k^S - V_k^U$. Direct substitution into \eqref{eq:conference-risks}
gives
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_R - \Delta_A = \frac{1 - 2q}{2}\bigl\{(1 - q)\Delta V_{RR} + \ell\Delta V_{CR}\bigr\}, \label{eq:conference-objectives}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta_A$ and $\Delta_R$ denote the aggregate and rare-risk differences.
The cross moment is the sum of the product of the coefficient biases and the
covariance of their errors.

\begin{theorem}[Objective disagreement]
  \label{thm:conference-c2}
  Take $q = .02$, $\ell \in[.9,1]$, $\beta^* = (2,1)^\top$, $h = .01$, $B = 1$,
  $k = M = 100$, and $\sigma^2 \in[3.8,4.1]$. Both U and S are mean-square
  stable, and uniformly over these intervals,
  \begin{align*}
    .00077 & \leq R_k^S - R_k^U \leq .00194,         \\
    -.0983 & \leq R_{R,k}^S - R_{R,k}^U \leq -.0233.
  \end{align*}
  Moreover, $V_{CC,k}^S > V_{CC,k}^U$ and $V_{RR,k}^S > V_{RR,k}^U$.
\end{theorem}

SD scaling increases each coefficient's mean squared error but improves
rare-presence prediction. At $\ell = 1$ and $\sigma^2 = 4$,
\[
  \Delta V \approx \begin{pmatrix}
                     .022743  & -.615146 \\
                     -.615146 & .470335
                   \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The reduction in the cross moment offsets the larger individual errors when
predicting for the rare subgroup. Aggregate risk gives this moment weight $.01$,
whereas rare risk gives it weight $.49$. Looking only at the individual
coefficient errors would therefore miss the improvement in rare prediction.

We prove both theorems using rational interval arithmetic with outward rounding
over the full parameter boxes. Appendix~\ref{sec:comparison-proofs} gives the
recurrences and enclosure arguments. The results apply to the specified training
procedures, whereas Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} compares risks after
optimizing over all constant rates and deterministic stops. Adaptive stopping
and the choice of an optimal normalization remain outside these results. The
identity relating the two risk objectives is elementary.

\section{From calibrated comparisons to selected procedures}
\label{sec:conference-experiments}

We use three experimental settings to examine how the comparisons change with
the training and selection procedure. We first compute exact moments for SGD
with fresh observations, then sample with replacement from noisy training sets,
and finally evaluate predictions on held-out records from a categorical dataset.
The observation budget counts every sampled training row, including repeated
visits, and we select final iterates from stored checkpoints. These experiments
preceded Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} and motivated its formulation.
A prospective test of its predicted orderings remains open.

\subsection{Rate and stopping adjustments}

The exact-moment study covers 228 binary laws, 51,048 training configurations,
and 2,571,800 endpoints. We vary prevalence, dependence, signal, noise, learning
rate, batch size, and stopping, comparing both equal update counts and equal
observation counts. In the primary adjusted comparison, we select the
configuration with the lowest exact risk among mean-square stable candidates and
initialization. We use a coarse rate grid from $1.953125 \times 10^{-6}$ to
$.64$ and check its resolution separately on 16 laws. Selection uses the known
test distribution, so this is an oracle comparison.

Both original anchor comparisons disappear after these adjustments.
Table~\ref{tab:conference-adjustment} shows the rare-risk differences at the
independent anchor of Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c1}, before and after
selecting the rate and stopping count. S no longer has higher risk at the late
$B = 1$ stop, with selected rates $h_U = .004$ and $h_S = .0003125$. At the
dependent anchor of Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c2}, selection favors U under
both objectives. Reversals and disagreements between objectives remain for other
laws. These results illustrate why Theorems~\ref{thm:conference-c1}
and~\ref{thm:conference-c2} apply to the fixed calibration. The separate oracle
comparison in Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} establishes orderings that
persist after optimizing over all constant rates and deterministic stops on its
two signal families.

dominated by extreme outcomes, a paired Monte Carlo covariance diagnostic also

investigation conditional on rare-feature absence. The exact comparisons among
stable candidates remain available, but these diagnostics prevent a claim of
complete agreement with simulation.

\subsection{Estimated preprocessing and validation selection}

We next use eight binary laws with 128 independent replications per law,
$n = 256$ training observations, and 128 validation observations. We compare
known preprocessing, estimated scales, training centering with a fixed or fitted
intercept, and uncentered predictors with a fitted intercept. The feature
divisors are $1$ for no scaling, $\sqrt{v_j}$ for SD scaling, and
$\sqrt{v_j + 0.01}$ for damped scaling \eqref{eq:conference-damped}. Estimated
variances use denominator $n$. Constant training columns are frozen at zero
coefficient with divisor one. Fitted intercepts start at the training response
mean. Appendix~\ref{sec:practical} gives further protocol details.

Each scaling and preprocessing arm receives the same five rate factors
\[
  \eta = \frac{a_0}{\max(L,10^{-12})},\qquad a_0 \in \{.001,.01,.05,.2,.8\},
\]
where $L$ is the largest empirical curvature, including the intercept coordinate
when fitted. This calibration does not guarantee stochastic stability. We use
batches $B \in \{1,16\}$ and observation caps $M \in \{128,512,2048\}$. We
compare fixed $a_0 = .05$, $B = 16$ with validation selection of the rate and
batch size, or of the rate, batch size, and stopping count. Initialization is
also eligible. The ten paths for each preprocessing arm and scaling rule cost
$10M$ processed observations at cap $M$, with checkpoints sharing paths. We use
no test outcomes during selection and do not refit the selected models.

For the independent equal-signal law at $q = .02$ and $\sigma^2 = 3$, centering
the predictors and fitting the intercept gives fixed-configuration rare-risk
differences of $-.1653$ (paired MCSE $.0295$) at $M = 128$ and $+.0901$
($.0697$) at $M = 2048$. The early advantage is clear, while the late
disadvantage is within two MCSE of zero. At the latter budget, the rare-only
signal favors S by $.2320$ ($.0233$), while the common-only signal favors U by
$.1272$ ($.0159$). These sampled-data results use empirical rate calibration, so
they do not extend the exact population theorems.

categorical-task comparisons described below.

\paragraph{When validation contains no rare cases.}

At $q = .002$, the two anchor laws have no rare validation observations in 106
and 105 of 128 replications. In these cases, the declared selection rule falls
back to aggregate loss. One selected SD fit with centered predictors has a rare
coefficient of about $-6.719 \times 10^{16}$ and validation half squared loss
$2.086$, yet its exact rare loss over the binary cells is about
$2.257 \times 10^{33}$. To investigate numerical error, we replay all eligible
trajectories in the worst selected replication for each of 24 combinations of
law and scaling, using 160-digit decimal arithmetic. All 24 selections remain
unchanged, so the detected floating-point cancellation does not explain this
failure. We retain the extreme finite outcomes and 151 failed synthetic
endpoints in the archive. Means dominated by these extremes do not precisely
estimate population risk.

\subsection{A sparse categorical prediction task}

We use squared loss and all 22 categorical predictors to predict the recorded

consists of mushrooms with green spore-print color, which accounts for 72 of

categories to zero blocks. We store the design sparsely and implement centering
algebraically. Coefficients are reported on the original feature scale, but
depend on the optimizer because the full one-hot design is rank deficient. We do
not clip predictions.

includes variance scaling, whose rate boundary is addressed by the four-rule
extension above. We give the full results and limitations in
Appendix~\ref{sec:mushroom-sensitivity}.

\section{Discussion}

Feature normalization affects the best attainable rare-subgroup risk even after
optimizing over all nonnegative constant rates and deterministic stops within a
common budget. Standardization has lower risk on the specified rare-signal
family, and unscaled training on the common-signal family. The gaps persist as
prevalence decreases while the expected number of rare observations stays of
order one. These sufficient conditions do not locate a general boundary between
the orderings.

The supporting comparisons show how standardization can reduce bias in the rare
coefficient while increasing noise, and how dependence can make aggregate and
rare-presence objectives favor different rules. Equalizing mean contraction
under independence does not guarantee lower risk. Interpretation requires the
learning rate, batch size, stopping count, and prediction objective alongside
the normalization rule.

Mixed binary and continuous predictors raise a further question: which changes
in a continuous predictor should match an absence-to-presence contrast? Even a
Gaussian assumption does not determine the appropriate comparison of effects.
Gaussian and rare Bernoulli predictors also have different fourth moments after
standardization, which affect the variability and stability of SGD updates.
Extending our risk comparisons to mixed predictors would require accounting for
these differences as well as choosing scales.

Tuning removes both original anchor comparisons. With estimated preprocessing
and validation selection, absent features and noisy validation add uncertainty.
On Mushroom, expanding the rate search removes the resolved large-sample SD
rare-risk advantage under aggregate selection, while damping retains its
advantage. The four-rule extension resolves the remaining rate boundaries on
this dataset. Variance scaling has higher rare risk in the primary comparison,
with an unresolved aggregate difference. Some synthetic selections still reach
rate boundaries, and numerical checks disagree on extreme paths.

Several fixed damping offsets improve risk, but rare validation produces a
severe failure in the smaller sample. The original offset $0.01$ remains a
heuristic. Selecting an offset would add tuning effort to the comparison.
Validation cannot directly assess performance for an absent group.

Our theoretical comparison assumes two independent features, known population
preprocessing, a fixed intercept, one fresh observation per update, and the
stated signal, prevalence, and budget ranges. The empirical searches are finite.
Estimated scales and validation selection remain open. Our results concern fixed
diagonal scaling; adaptive optimizers require further analysis. The next
empirical step is to declare the predicted orderings before evaluating new
synthetic configurations and an additional categorical task. Broader
normalization recommendations also require studying how selection depends on
rare-group coverage.

\section{Proof of the comparison after oracle tuning}
\label{sec:predictive-proofs}

We prove Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} using exact error moments. We
first restrict the rate parameter in \eqref{eq:conference-oracle-rates} to
$\alpha \in[0,1]$. For each signal family, we then bound all larger rates for
the method with higher risk, showing that none can reverse the ordering. Two
independent implementations, \texttt{experiments/check\_predictive\_theorem.py}
and \texttt{experiments/check\_predictive\_review.py}, check the rational
certificates and the reductions of the moment equations. These checks run with
\texttt{make check-predictive-theorem} and \texttt{make check-predictive-review}
in the locked environment.

The proof for bounded rates has undergone a separate AI-assisted mathematical
review. For the extension to unrestricted rates, we have local algebraic checks
and a Lean formalization of the lower bounds conditional on the moment
recurrence. Independent review of that extension and human verification of the
complete theorem remain open in this working draft.

\subsection{Exact moments}

By homogeneity, we may take $\sigma^2 = 1$. Put $s = (\beta_C^*)^2$,
$r = (\beta_R^*)^2$, $Q = 10^{-4}$, and $\tau = Mb$. We write
$C_k = \E[(\beta_{C,k}-\beta_C^*)^2]$ and $H_k = \E[(\beta_{R,k}-\beta_R^*)^2]$.
Independence gives
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k} = C_k/8 + (1 - q)^2H_k/2. \label{eq:predictive-risk}
\end{equation}
The required feature moments are $x_C^2 = 1/4$, $\E x_R^2 = b$,
$\E x_R^4 = b(1 - 3b)$, and $\E x_C^2x_R^2 = b/4$. Mixed moments with an odd
power of $x_C$ vanish. Expanding the squared update and using the conditional
noise assumptions therefore gives
\begin{equation}
  \binom{C_{k+1}}{H_{k+1}} = \begin{pmatrix}
                               A           & 4u^2b \\
                               b\alpha^2/4 & D_b
                             \end{pmatrix}\binom{C_k}{H_k}
                             + \binom{4u^2}{b\alpha^2},\qquad \binom{C_0}{H_0}
                           = \binom{s}{r}, \label{eq:predictive-moments}
\end{equation}
where $A = (1 - u)^2$, $D_b = 1 - b\alpha(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha)$, and
$u = \alpha/4$ for U or $u = b\alpha$ for S. The diagonal equations and the risk
do not involve cross error moments, so changing the signs of the coefficients
leaves the risk unchanged. Every matrix entry is nonnegative for every
$\alpha \geq 0$, since the diagonal entries are expectations of squared update
coefficients.

For $0 < \alpha \leq 1$, the row sums are
\[
  1 - u\{2 - u(1 + 4b)\} < 1,\qquad 1 - b\alpha\{2 - 5\alpha/4 + 3b\alpha\} < 1.
\]
The homogeneous moments therefore contract, and the moments contributed by noise
stay bounded. Since the cross moment is bounded by $\sqrt{C_kH_k}$, all positive
rates that we use below as candidates for the method with lower risk are
mean-square stable. At $\alpha = 0$, the predictor stays at initialization.

\subsection{Dominant rare signal on the bounded rate interval}

Suppose $r = 1$ and $s \leq 1/100$. For U, we have $A \leq D_b$ because the
common contraction factor falls below one by at least $7\alpha/16$, whereas the
rare factor falls below one by at most $2b\alpha$. Dropping nonnegative terms in
\eqref{eq:predictive-moments} and summing the noise terms gives, with
$z = D_b^k$,
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k}^U \geq \frac12\left[ (1 - q)^2z + \left\{ \frac{(1 - q)^2\alpha}{2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha} + \frac{\alpha}{8 - \alpha} \right\}(1 - z) \right]. \label{eq:predictive-u-lower}
\end{equation}
For the common-noise term, we use $1 - A^k \geq 1 - D_b^k$. The bound also holds
directly at zero rate. Since the right side is affine in $z$, we can bound its
minimum over $k \leq M$ by its values at $z = 1$ and $z = D_b^M$.

Set $d(\alpha) = \alpha(2 - \alpha)$, $Z = e^{-\tau d(\alpha)}$, and
\[
  N(\alpha) = \frac{\alpha}{2 - \alpha}
              + \frac{\alpha}{8 - \alpha},\qquad F_U^-(\alpha,\tau)
            = \tfrac12[Z + N(\alpha)(1 - Z)].
\]
Writing $d_b = d(\alpha) + 3b\alpha^2$, the logarithm series yields
\[
  0 \leq -\frac{\log(1 - bd_b)}b - d(\alpha)
    \leq b\left[ 3 + \frac{(1 + 3Q)^2}{2\{1 - Q(1 + 3Q)\}} \right]
    < 4b.
\]
Hence $|D_b^M - Z| \leq 16q$. The initial and noise coefficients in
\eqref{eq:predictive-u-lower} differ from $1$ and $N(\alpha)$ by at most $2q$
and $5q$, respectively, and $|1 - N(\alpha)| \leq 1$. It follows that
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,k}^U \geq \min\{1/2,F_U^-(\alpha,\tau)\} - 11q, \qquad 0
            \leq \alpha
            \leq 1,\quad 0
            \leq k
            \leq M. \label{eq:predictive-u-transfer}
\end{equation}

For S, we use $Y_k = C_k/4 + H_k$ and
$\widetilde D = 1 - bd(\alpha) + b^2\alpha^2$. The exact recurrence gives
\[
  Y_{k+1} = \frac{\widetilde D}{4}C_k
            + (D_b + b^3\alpha^2)H_k
            + b\alpha^2(1 + b)
          \leq \widetilde D Y_k + b\alpha^2(1 + b),
\]
since $\widetilde D - (D_b + b^3\alpha^2) = b^2\alpha^2(4 - b) \geq 0$. Using
$R_{R,M}^S \leq Y_M/2$ and summing the geometric series gives
\[
  R_{R,M}^S \leq \frac12\left[ (r + s/4)\widetilde D^M + \frac{\alpha(1 + b)}{2 - \alpha - b\alpha}(1 - \widetilde D^M) \right].
\]
Define
\[
  F_S(\alpha,\tau;s,r) = \tfrac12\left[ (r + s/4)Z + \frac{\alpha}{2 - \alpha}(1 - Z) \right].
\]
The logarithm remainder and plateau difference satisfy
\[
  |\widetilde D^M - Z| \leq 4b\left[ 1 + \frac1{2(1 - Q)} \right],\qquad 0
                       \leq \frac{\alpha(1 + b)}{2 - \alpha - b\alpha}
                            - \frac{\alpha}{2 - \alpha}
                       \leq \frac{2b}{1 - Q}.
\]
Consequently, for the rare-signal family,
\begin{equation}
  R_{R,M}^S \leq F_S(\alpha,\tau;1/100,1) + 5q. \label{eq:predictive-s-transfer}
\end{equation}
The unrounded coefficient of $q$ is at most
$\{(401/400)4[1 + 1/(2(1 - Q))] + 2/(1 - Q)\}/2 < 5$.

We choose the stable candidate $\alpha_S = 2/(\tau + 4) \in[1/4,2/5]$ and
$k = M$. We next construct a rational certificate for the bound
\begin{equation}
  \min\{1/2,F_U^-(\alpha,\tau)\} - F_S(2/(\tau + 4),\tau;1/100,1)
  \geq 1/100 \quad\text{on }[0,1]
       \times[1,4] . \label{eq:predictive-certificate}
\end{equation}
Both $d$ and $N$ increase on $[0,1]$. On a rectangle
$[a_0,a_1] \times[t_0,t_1]$, we put $N_0 = N(a_0)$ and bound $Z$ between
$e^{-t_1d(a_1)}$ and $e^{-t_0d(a_0)}$. Since $(N + (1 - N)Z)/2$ increases with
$N$, we obtain a lower bound by using $N_0$ and the lower endpoint for $Z$ if
$N_0 \leq 1$, or the upper endpoint otherwise. We then take the minimum with
$1/2$. The S candidate has plateau $1/(\tau + 3)$ and exponent
$4\tau(\tau + 3)/(\tau + 4)^2$. Its risk decreases with $\tau$, so its value at
$t_0$ gives an upper bound on the rectangle.

For rational $x \in[0,4]$, put $P_{18}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{18}x^j/j!$. Positivity
and the ratio of successive omitted terms give
\begin{equation}
  P_{18}(x)
  \leq e^x
  \leq P_{18}(x)
       + \frac{x^{19}/19!}{1 - x/20}. \label{eq:predictive-exp-certificate}
\end{equation}
Taking reciprocals gives rational enclosures for $e^{-x}$. Starting from the
whole rectangle, the theorem checker subdivides each unresolved rectangle into
four equal rectangles. It accepts a leaf only when the lower margin is at least
$1/100$. The 5,035 accepted leaves have maximum depth nine and total area
exactly three, with minimum rational margin above $0.01000005$. The independent
review checker uses a different exponential enclosure and subdivision, yielding
3,925 leaves. Both constructions certify the inequality over the continuous
domain.

Combining \eqref{eq:predictive-u-transfer}, \eqref{eq:predictive-s-transfer},
and \eqref{eq:predictive-certificate}, we find that every U candidate in the
bounded rate interval has risk exceeding that of the S candidate by at least
\[
  1/100 - 16q \geq 0.0084 > 0.007.
\]

\subsection{Dominant common signal on the bounded rate interval}

Suppose $s = 1$ and $r \leq 1/100$. For S, the common mean error is
$-(1 - b\alpha)^k\beta_C^*$. If $\alpha \leq 1/4$, Jensen's inequality and the
logarithm bound give
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \tfrac18(1 - b\alpha)^{2k}
            \geq \tfrac18\exp\{-2/(1 - Q/4)\}
            > 1/60.
\]
If $1/4 \leq \alpha \leq 1$, put $z = (1 - b\alpha)^k$. Since
$D_b \leq 1 - b\alpha$ and $\alpha/(2 - \alpha + 3b\alpha) \geq \alpha/2$,
common squared bias and direct rare observation noise yield
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq z^2/8 + \frac{(1 - q)^2\alpha}{4}(1 - z)
            \geq z^2/8 + \frac3{50}(1 - z)
            \geq \frac{33}{625}
            > \frac1{60}.
\]
We obtain the penultimate inequality by minimizing the quadratic over $[0,1]$.

For U, we put $h = \sqrt b \leq 1/100$, choose $\alpha_U = 4h$, and stop at $M$.
Then $u = h$. Write $H_{\max} = \max_{0 \leq j \leq M}H_j$. Geometric summation
in the common equation of \eqref{eq:predictive-moments} gives
\[
  C_j \leq A^j + \frac{4h}{2 - h} + \frac{4h^3}{2 - h}H_{\max}.
\]
To obtain an upper bound, we drop the contraction in the rare coordinate and sum
this inequality. Using $M \leq 4/h^2$ and $\sum_{j \geq 0}A^j = 1/\{h(2 - h)\}$
then gives
\[
  H_{\max} \leq r + \frac{68h^3}{2 - h} + 64h^2 + \frac{64h^5}{2 - h}H_{\max}.
\]
Thus
\[
  H_{\max} \leq H_*(h)
           := \frac{1/100 + 68h^3/(2 - h) + 64h^2}{1 - 64h^5/(2 - h)}.
\]
Using $M \geq 1/h^2$ in the common bound now gives
\[
  R_{R,M}^U \leq \frac{e^{-2/h}}8
                 + \frac{h}{2(2 - h)}
                 + \left\{ \frac12 + \frac{h^3}{2(2 - h)} \right\}H_*(h)
            < 0.01072966
            < \frac{11}{1000}.
\]
Each term increases with $h$ on this interval. At $h = 1/100$, the checker
verifies the bound rationally using $e^{-200} \leq 2^{-200}$. It verifies the
lower exponential bound above using \eqref{eq:predictive-exp-certificate}. Every
S candidate in the bounded rate interval therefore has risk exceeding that of
the U candidate by more than $1/60 - 11/1000 > 1/200$.

\subsection{Removing the rate cap}

For the rare-signal family, $H_0 = 1$. Put $m_b = 1/(1 + 3b)$. For every
$\alpha \geq 1$,
\[
  D_bm_b + b\alpha^2 - m_b = \frac{2b\alpha(\alpha - 1)}{1 + 3b} \geq 0.
\]
Dropping the nonnegative common-to-rare term in \eqref{eq:predictive-moments}
and using induction gives $H_k \geq m_b$ at every finite stop. Hence
\[
  R_{R,k}^U(\alpha) \geq \frac{(1 - q)^2}{2(1 + 3b)}
                    \geq \frac{(1 - Q)^2}{2(1 + 3Q)}
                    > \frac{49}{100}.
\]
The original S candidate remains below $1/3$, since
\[
  R_{R,M}^S(\alpha_S) \leq F_S(2/(\tau + 4),\tau;1/100,1) + 5q
                      \leq F_S(2/5,1;1/100,1) + 5Q
                      < \frac13.
\]
Rational exponential enclosures certify the last bound. Every added U candidate
therefore has risk exceeding that of the S candidate by more than
$49/100 - 1/3 > 7/1000$.

For the common-signal family, $C_0 = 1$ and $H_0 \geq 0$. Under S, let
$A = (1 - b\alpha)^2$. The identity $D_b - A = b\alpha^2(1 - 4b) \geq 0$ and
positivity imply
\[
  C_k \geq A^k,\qquad H_k
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}D_b^j
      \geq b\alpha^2\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}A^j.
\]
If $A \geq 1$, the common error gives $R_{R,k}^S \geq 1/8$. Otherwise
$0 < b\alpha < 2$, and geometric summation gives
\[
  R_{R,k}^S \geq \frac{A^k}{8}
                 + \frac{(1 - q)^2\alpha}{2(2 - b\alpha)}(1 - A^k).
\]
For $\alpha \geq 1$, the second coefficient is at least $(1 - q)^2/4 > 1/8$.
Thus $R_{R,k}^S \geq 1/8$ for every added rate and stop, including $k = 0$. The
stable U candidate remains below $11/1000$, giving a gap greater than
$1/8 - 11/1000 > 1/200$.

For each signal family, we have now bounded every candidate of the method with
higher risk against one admissible stable candidate of the other method. Taking
the infima over the enlarged searches proves both inequalities. Restricting each
search to stable rates preserves the bounds and the candidates. Multiplying by
$\sigma^2$ restores the stated gaps. The argument requires neither stability of
all added rates nor attainment of the unrestricted infima.

\section{Proofs of the normalization-risk comparisons}
\label{sec:comparison-proofs}

In this appendix, we derive the moment recurrences and approximation bounds used
to prove Theorems~\ref{thm:conference-c1} and~\ref{thm:conference-c2}. We then
describe the interval certificates. Throughout, we use the binary model, fresh
sampling, and population preprocessing of Section~\ref{sec:conference-setting}.
The implementation is in
\texttt{experiments/check\_normalization\_risk\_theorem.py}. Running
\texttt{make check-normalization-risk} in the locked project environment checks
both the certificates and independent numerical calculations. We still need to
package an anonymized reproducibility archive for submission.

\subsection{Exact moments and the variance decomposition}

Write $D = \eta P = \diag(d_C,d_R)$ and
$s_k = (V_{CC,k},V_{CR,k},V_{RR,k})^\top$. Set
\[
  t_B = c^2 + \frac{ab - c^2}{B},\qquad m_B
      = c\left( b + \frac{1 - 4b}{B} \right).
\]
The exact recurrence is $s_{k+1} = L_Ds_k + \sigma^2g_D$, where
\[
  L_D
  = \begin{pmatrix}
      (1 - d_Ca)^2    & -2d_C(1 - d_Ca)c                 & d_C^2t_B                        \\
      -d_R(1 - d_Ca)c & (1 - d_Ca)(1 - d_Rb) + d_Cd_Rt_B & -d_Cc + d_Cd_Rm_B               \\
      d_R^2t_B        & -2d_Rc + 2d_R^2m_B               & (1 - d_Rb)^2 + d_R^2b(1 - 4b)/B
    \end{pmatrix},
\]
\[
  g_D = \frac1B(d_C^2a,d_Cd_Rc,d_R^2b)^\top,\qquad s_0
      = ((\beta_C^*)^2,\beta_C^*\beta_R^*,(\beta_R^*)^2)^\top.
\]
To verify the recurrence, let $\widehat C$ be the average second moment in a
batch. The squared common coordinate is always $a$, and the binary law gives
\[
  \E x_R^4 = b(1 - 3b),\quad \E x_C^2x_R^2 = ab,\quad \E x_Cx_R^3 = c(1 - 3b).
\]
Consequently, $\E\widehat c^2 = t_B$, $\E(\widehat c\widehat b) = m_B$, and
$\E\widehat b^2 = b^2 + b(1 - 4b)/B$. Expanding
$\E[(I-D\widehat C)V_k(I-\widehat CD)]$ yields $L_D$. The conditional mean-zero
noise assumption removes the cross terms, and the added noise has covariance
$\sigma^2DCD/B$. Because each batch is fresh, its update matrix is independent
of the previous error.

To prove \eqref{eq:conference-decomposition}, we run the noiseless recursion on
the same feature stream. Its mean error is $e_k = -(I - DC)^k\beta^*$ and its
covariance is $A_k \succeq 0$. Conditional on all sampled features, the
component due to noise has mean zero, so its cross moment with the noiseless
component vanishes. Its covariance is $\sigma^2N_k \succeq 0$, which proves the
decomposition. Equivalently, if
$\mathcal T_D(V) = \E[(I-D\widehat C)V(I-\widehat CD)]$, then
\[
  V_k = \mathcal T_D^k(\beta^*\beta^{*\top})
        + \frac{\sigma^2}{B}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\mathcal T_D^j(DCD).
\]

\subsection{A scalar approximation with a controlled remainder}

Under independence, the diagonal moments satisfy a closed recurrence with matrix
\[
  K_D = \begin{pmatrix}
          A & e   \\
          f & D_0
        \end{pmatrix},\quad A
      = (1 - h)^2,\quad e
      = \frac{d_C^2ab}{B},\quad f
      = \frac{d_R^2ab}{B},\quad D_0
      = (1 - d_Rb)^2 + \frac{d_R^2b(1 - 4b)}B.
\]
Here $D_0$ is a scalar, distinct from $D$. All entries are nonnegative. Define
\[
  w = \tfrac12(a,(1 - q)^2)^\top,\quad v_0
    = ((\beta_C^*)^2,(\beta_R^*)^2)^\top,\quad g
    = (d_C^2a,d_R^2b)^\top/B,
\]
and let $E_0 = \max(e,f)$ and $\|K_D\|_\infty \leq \rho < 1$. For
$\phi_k(z) = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}z^j$, define
\[
  \widetilde R_{R,k} = w_C\{A^k(\beta_C^*)^2 + \sigma^2g_C\phi_k(A)\}
                       + w_R\{D_0^k(\beta_R^*)^2 + \sigma^2g_R\phi_k(D_0)\}.
\]
For every $k \geq 1$,
\begin{equation}
  0
  \leq R_{R,k} - \widetilde R_{R,k}
  \leq E_F + \sigma^2E_N,\qquad E_F
  = \|w\|_1\|v_0\|_\infty E_0k\rho^{k-1},\quad E_N
  = \frac{\|w\|_1\|g\|_\infty E_0}{(1 - \rho)^2}. \label{eq:comparison-remainder}
\end{equation}
Indeed, with $K_0 = \diag(A,D_0)$ and $E = K_D - K_0 \geq 0$ entrywise,
\[
  K_D^k - K_0^k = \sum_{j=0}^{k-1}K_D^{k-1-j}EK_0^j \geq 0.
\]
The difference has infinity norm at most $E_0k\rho^{k-1}$. Multiplying by $w$
and $v_0$ proves the homogeneous bound. We obtain the noise bound by applying
the same identity to the noise sum and using
$\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}j\rho^{j-1} \leq (1 - \rho)^{-2}$.

The common-coordinate terms cancel when we subtract the U approximation from the
S approximation. Write
\[
  \widetilde F = w_R(\beta_R^*)^2(D_S^k - D_U^k),\qquad \widetilde N
               = w_R\{g_{R,S}\phi_k(D_S) - g_{R,U}\phi_k(D_U)\},
\]
where $D_S,D_U$ are the respective scalar $D_0$ values. For the exact difference
$F + \sigma^2N$, subtracting the two nonnegative remainders gives
\[
  F_- := \widetilde F - E_{F,U} \leq F \leq \widetilde F + E_{F,S} = :F_+,
\]
\[
  N_- := \widetilde N - E_{N,U} \leq N \leq \widetilde N + E_{N,S} = :N_+.
\]
When $F_+ < 0 < N_-$, division by positive denominators gives
\eqref{eq:conference-threshold}. In particular, $F_+ + \sigma^2N_+ < 0$
guarantees lower rare risk for S, whereas $F_- + \sigma^2N_- > 0$ guarantees
lower rare risk for U. The homogeneous approximation includes variation from
rare-feature sampling through $D_0$, in addition to the squared mean error.

\subsection{Certificate for Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c1}}

We partition each of the $q$ and $h$ intervals into eight equal closed pieces.
On each of the resulting 64 rectangles, we evaluate the preceding bounds using
interval arithmetic and $(\beta_C^*)^2,(\beta_R^*)^2 \in[.9025,1.1025]$. For
each configuration, we use upper bounds on $\rho = \max(A + e,D_0 + f)$ and
$E_0 = \max(e,f)$. This gives $\rho < .999719$ throughout. The remaining cross
moment is multiplied at each step by
\[
  \gamma = (1 - d_Ca)(1 - d_Rb) + d_Cd_Rab/B
\]
with no added noise term. The certificate verifies $0 \leq \gamma < 1$ on every
rectangle. The full homogeneous second-moment map is therefore stable, and the
moments remain bounded when we include the fixed finite noise term.

Every rectangle satisfies $F_+ < 0 < N_-$. Taking the hull of the resulting
$F + \sigma^2N$ intervals gives the bounds in Table~\ref{tab:conference-c1}. In
table order, the enclosures for the noise threshold are $[10.46,17.76]$,
$[.53,1.405]$, $[112.61,184.22]$, and $[5.69,14.49]$. They imply the four signs
uniformly whenever $1.405 < \sigma^2 < 5.69$. The narrower interval in the
theorem gives the displayed risk-gap bounds. Because the rectangles are closed,
the certificate also covers their shared boundaries.

\subsection{Outward-rounded arithmetic}

We represent each interval endpoint as an integer multiple of $10^{-60}$ and
read inputs as rational strings or integers. Addition is exact. For
multiplication, we take the minimum and maximum of all four endpoint products
and round outward using integer division. If $[l,u]$ stores the integer
endpoints of a positive denominator interval, we enclose its reciprocal by
\[
  \left[ \frac{\lfloor10^{120}/u\rfloor}{10^{60}}, \frac{\lceil10^{120}/l\rceil}{10^{60}} \right].
\]
We reduce negative denominators to this case and compute powers and finite sums
by repeated squaring and addition. Induction over these operations shows that
each interval contains the exact rational expression. All acceptance checks
compare integer endpoints, including the check that they lie within the reported
decimal bounds. We convert to floating point only for display. The enclosure
calculations therefore cover the full parameter boxes.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c2}}

To establish stability, we use the fact that a feature vector with
$\|z\|^2 \leq L$ satisfies
\[
  \E[(I-\eta zz^\top)^2] \preceq I - \eta(2 - \eta L)H, \qquad H = \E[zz^\top].
\]
If $H \succ 0$ and $0 < \eta < 2/L$, this contracts expected squared error. For
U, take $L = .25 + .98^2 = 1.2104$, $\eta = .04$, and use
$\lambda_{\min}(C) \geq .0196 - .01 = .0096$. For S, $L = 50$, $\eta = .01$, and
\[
  H = \begin{pmatrix}
        1      & \ell/7 \\
        \ell/7 & 1
      \end{pmatrix},\qquad \lambda_{\min}(H)
    \geq 6/7.
\]
These sufficient stability bounds hold throughout the claimed interval.

To bound the risks, we augment $L_D$ with the unit-noise column $g_D$ and a
final row $(0,0,0,1)$. Raising this matrix to the $100$th power gives the
homogeneous map and finite noise sum. The aggregate and rare risk weights on
$s_k$ are
\[
  w_A = (a/2,c,b/2)^\top,\qquad w_R = (a/2,\ell(1 - q)/2,(1 - q)^2/2)^\top.
\]
We partition $[.9,1]$ into 128 equal closed intervals. On each interval, we use
the same integer arithmetic to enclose the two augmented powers, their action on
$s_0 = (4,2,1)^\top$, and the contrasts $F_W + \sigma^2N_W$ for
$\sigma^2 \in[3.8,4.1]$. Signed matrix entries use all four endpoint products;
no entrywise positivity is assumed. The union satisfies both risk enclosures in
the theorem. Before risk weighting, the same powers certify positive diagonal
contrasts and a negative cross-moment contrast throughout. This proves the
coefficient MSE inequalities as well.

\subsection{Checks distinct from the certificates}

We independently check the three-moment formulas against gradient updates by
enumerating ordered batches and short paths. We also compare them with a
separate full moment operator at 64 endpoints, including signals with mixed
signs, boundaries of the dependence range, an interior law, and $B = 1,2$. These
floating-point checks validate the implementations. The interval arithmetic
above certifies the inequalities over the continuous parameter boxes. Neither
check establishes priority or extends the results to procedures outside the
stated comparisons.

\section{Estimated preprocessing and validation selection}
\label{sec:practical}

We next examine whether the effects in the controlled comparisons remain visible
when we sample training data, estimate preprocessing, and select configurations
by validation. We use a finite grid of candidates, so this experiment does not
extend the population theorems to estimated scales. The full protocol, results,
and reproducible artifacts are recorded in \texttt{notes/practical-selection.md}
and \texttt{notes/practical-selection-results.md}.

For the synthetic experiment, we use eight feasible binary laws with 128
independent replications per law, 256 training observations, and 128 independent
validation observations. We include independent and nested signals at $q = 0.02$
and $q = 0.002$, signals with opposite signs under nested and disjoint
dependence, and rare-only and common-only signals. We compare known
preprocessing with estimated scales, training centering with a fixed or fitted
intercept, and uncentered predictors with a fitted intercept. We estimate
variances with denominator $n$ and give constant training columns divisor one
and coefficient zero. Absent features therefore cannot acquire a fitted effect
in the estimated preprocessing arms. Fitted intercepts start at the training
response mean, while the population-oracle arms start at the true centered
intercept of zero. SGD samples with replacement from a fixed noisy training set,
so revisiting an observation also reuses its noise.

The divisors are $1$, $\sqrt{v_j}$, and $\sqrt{v_j + 0.01}$. For each scaling
and preprocessing arm, we use five rates $\eta = a/\max(L,10^{-12})$,
$a \in \{0.001,0.01,0.05,0.2,0.8\}$, and two batch sizes. Here $L$ is the
largest empirical curvature, including the unscaled intercept coordinate when
fitted. This calibration does not guarantee stochastic stability. We use batches
$B \in \{1,16\}$ and observation caps $M \in \{128,512,2048\}$. We retain
initialization and every checkpoint, and compare fixed $a = 0.05$, $B = 16$ with
validation selection of the rate and batch size, or of the rate, batch size, and
stopping count. We select by either aggregate or rare-presence validation half
squared loss. When the rare validation group is empty, its selection rule falls
back to aggregate loss. We use no test outcomes in selection and do not refit
selected models on validation data. Each combination of scaling and
preprocessing uses ten trajectories, costing $10M$ processed observations at cap
$M$, with checkpoints sharing paths.

Differences in how quickly the coefficients are learned remain visible with
estimated preprocessing. For the independent $(1,1)$ signal at $q = 0.02$ and
noise variance three, the fixed configuration gives an SD-minus-none rare excess
half squared loss of $-0.1653$ (paired MCSE $0.0295$) at $M = 128$ and $+0.0901$
($0.0697$) at $M = 2048$ with training centering and a fitted intercept. The
early advantage is clear, while the later disadvantage is within two MCSE of
zero. At the latter budget, the rare-only signal favors SD by $0.2320$
($0.0233$), whereas the common-only signal favors no scaling by $0.1272$
($0.0159$). These observations concern the declared configurations with sampled
data, rather than the exact calibrations of the population comparisons.

Missing groups create a further problem. At $q = 0.002$, the two anchor laws
have no rare validation observations in 106 and 105 of 128 replications.
Validation can then select a finite but explosive rare coefficient because no
rare cases are available to reveal its error. One SD fit with centered
predictors has a rare coefficient of about $-6.719 \times 10^{16}$ and
validation half squared loss $2.086$, yet its exact rare loss over the binary
cells is about $2.257 \times 10^{33}$. We retain this path in the results. In a
separate investigation, we replay every eligible trajectory for the worst
selected replication in each of 24 combinations of law and scaling, using
160-digit decimal arithmetic. All 24 selections remain unchanged, so the
detected floating-point cancellation does not explain the failure. Means
dominated by these extreme finite outcomes do not precisely estimate population
risk. We also retain all 151 failed synthetic endpoints, flagged in the archive.

$42.6229$, median $.004108$, and maximum $1356.8142$, while rare loss has mean
$4.7479$ and maximum $150.2734$. The partition responsible for most of this loss
contains two green training examples and one green validation example. It
selects a finite path with $B = 16$, $M = 2048$, and rate factor $1.4$, whose

Variance scaling gives larger losses on the tested grid, but two primary
large-sample selections use the upper rate factor $6.4$. Across caps and
objectives, it reaches the upper boundary in 31 selections, counting repeated
partitions. No other rule reaches a rate boundary in this arm. The search

\section{Variance scaling across practical preprocessing arms}
\label{sec:variance-scaling-followup}

In a separately declared follow-up, we add variance scaling, $s_j = v_j$
($\delta = 1$), to every preprocessing arm of the sampled binary and Mushroom
studies. We reuse all eight binary laws with 128 replications per law, both
Mushroom sample sizes with 32 partitions per size, and the original training,
validation, and test samples. We use population variances only in the synthetic
arm with known preprocessing. All other arms use training variances with
denominator $n$. We keep the same treatment of constant columns and unknown
categories, as well as the same centering, initialization, and intercept rules.

We give no scaling, SD scaling, damping $.01$, and variance scaling the same 25
rate factors relative to empirical curvature. These comprise the earlier
17-factor Mushroom grid, two smaller factors $10^{-5}$ and $10^{-4}$, and six
successive doublings from $12.8$ through $409.6$. With two batch sizes, each
rule has nominal cost $50M$ processed observations per preprocessing arm and
replication or partition at cap $M$. We jointly select the rate, batch size, and
stopping count by aggregate or rare validation loss. The primary search uses the
original checkpoints, while a secondary search adds intermediate checkpoints. We
also retain the nested five-factor practical and 17-factor Mushroom searches to
compare results at the earlier tuning effort. Test outcomes enter neither
selection nor the choice of grid. We retain initialization, fallbacks for empty
groups, numerical failures, and extreme finite selections.

The motivation for variance scaling under the lasso concerns penalty weights on
the original coefficient scale. Here, we use no explicit penalty. The SGD
multiplier for an active coordinate is $\eta/s_j^2 = \eta/v_j^2$, and
calibrating by empirical curvature also changes $\eta$. We therefore compare how
the rules affect relative learning, noise, and stability under a finite budget.
The motivation from shrinkage does not establish which rule should have lower
prediction risk after tuning.

\paragraph{Mushroom results and search boundaries.}

At $n = 2048$ and $M = 8192$, aggregate selection with the original stops gives
variance scaling aggregate loss $.311988$ (MCSE $.201772$) and rare loss
$.051586$ ($.008319$) when predictors are centered and the intercept is fitted.
Its rare-loss differences relative to none, SD, and damping are $.045630$
($.008323$), $.046822$ ($.008189$), and $.050904$ ($.008322$), respectively.
Each exceeds two paired MCSE, while the aggregate differences do not. For
variance scaling, aggregate loss has median $.069802$ and maximum $6.505207$.
Selecting by rare validation raises its aggregate mean to $25.5720$ (MCSE
$8.0453$).

For the two primary selections at factor $6.4$, the newly tested neighbor $12.8$
gives finite but worse validation scores. Across both Mushroom sample sizes, all
preprocessing arms, caps, objectives, and both expanded stopping searches, no
selection with a positive budget reaches either grid boundary. With the original
stops, every selected predictor matches the nested 17-factor search. Sixty
candidate indices change because the deterministic tie rule assigns the same
initialization a lower factor. The refined stopping search selects some added
rates, up to $102.4$, and we report it separately.

\paragraph{Extreme synthetic outcomes and unresolved cases.}

We evaluate all eight laws and five preprocessing arms at equal effort. For the
rarer C2 law with centered predictors and a fitted intercept, aggregate
selection at cap $2048$ gives variance scaling an exact rare-risk median of
$.6836$, mean $1.727 \times 10^{30}$, MCSE $1.552 \times 10^{30}$, and maximum
$1.974 \times 10^{32}$. Extreme outcomes dominate these means, including those
for SD and damping, so they do not precisely estimate expected risk. Empty rare
validation groups can still conceal severe prediction errors. For the rare-only
signal, variance scaling gives aggregate mean $.01065$ ($.00311$) but rare mean
$.4122$ ($.1467$), compared with $.2326$ ($.0319$) for no scaling.

Across the two expanded stopping searches, synthetic selections reach the lower
rate boundary 106 times and the upper boundary 52 times, counting repeated
queries on the same replications. Only variance scaling reaches the upper
boundary, under known preprocessing in the two rarer anchor laws. Resolving the
Mushroom boundaries therefore leaves these synthetic cases open. Across both
stages, the archives retain 6,451,200 endpoints and 387,072 choices, including
1,031,540 failed endpoints on 330,918 failed paths.

\paragraph{Independent audit and numerical limits.}

The audit reconstructs every loss and selection, verifies all 179 preserved
files, and replays 100,271 paths in explicitly transformed coordinates.
Overlapping frozen coefficient paths reproduce exactly. Reconstructed losses
have maximum normwise discrepancy $1.36 \times 10^{-14}$ and leave every
selection unchanged. Mushroom passes the declared trajectory and prediction
tolerances. For synthetic data, however, the audit retains 63 discrepancies in
twelve replications, all of which have empty rare validation groups. These
comprise 60 checks of selected predictions, two first-failure times, and one
trajectory check. Repeated queries can refer to the same selected path. The
maximum normwise coefficient and prediction discrepancies are $.003747$ and
$.004184$, exceeding the declared tolerances of $2 \times 10^{-9}$ and
$10^{-8}$. All stored checkpoint statuses agree.

The transformed replay therefore does not certify these extreme synthetic paths.
We retain the original candidates and selections in the archive. The result note
records the affected configurations and explains how we extended the checker to
retain disagreements in failure times rather than stop at the first one. See
\path{notes/variance-scaling-followup-results.md} for the protocol, archives,
and reproduction commands. All earlier studies retain their frozen file hashes.
