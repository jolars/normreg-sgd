\section{Discussion}

Normalization changes how a finite training budget is distributed across
features. The main theorem shows that its effect on attainable rare-subgroup
risk can survive oracle tuning of every nonnegative constant learning rate and
every deterministic stop within the budget. Standardization wins on the stated
rare-signal family and unscaled training on the common-signal family. The gaps
remain positive as prevalence decreases while the expected rare observation
count stays of order one. This establishes a persistent comparison in a regime
with few rare observations, without a general boundary between the orderings.

In the supporting binary comparisons, standardization can reduce
rare-feature bias while increasing propagated noise, and dependence can make
aggregate and rare-presence prediction favor different rules. The same error
moments explain both comparisons. Their interpretation requires the rate, batch
size, stopping count, and prediction objective to be stated alongside the
normalization rule.

The experiments also show why a fixed comparison cannot establish an advantage
for a tuned method. Both original anchor comparisons disappear under the
declared oracle adjustments. Estimated preprocessing and validation selection
retain relative-learning effects, but introduce additional uncertainty from
absent features and noisy selection. Expanding the categorical rate search
removes the resolved large-sample SD rare-risk advantage under aggregate
selection, while damping's advantage persists. The four-rule extension includes
variance scaling and removes the remaining Mushroom rate edges. Variance retains
a primary rare-risk disadvantage, with an unresolved aggregate contrast.
Synthetic rate boundaries and numerical disagreements on extreme paths remain
explicit limitations. A separate fixed-offset comparison retains the main
damping benefit at several tested offsets, while exposing an extreme
small-sample failure under rare validation. The original offset $0.01$ remains a
heuristic, and selecting a damping level would require accounting for the
additional tuning effort. A rare-objective selection rule cannot directly assess
performance on rare observations that never appear in validation.

The oracle theorem assumes two independent features, known population
preprocessing, a fixed intercept, one fresh observation per update, and the
specified signal, prevalence, and budget ranges. It permits continuous constant
rates; the empirical studies use the stated finite searches. The population
theorems do not provide guarantees for estimated scales or validation-selected
configurations. Fixed diagonal scaling is also distinct from the evolving
preconditioners of adaptive
optimizers. The next empirical test should declare the predicted orderings
before evaluating new synthetic configurations and an additional categorical
task. A broader normalization recommendation would also require a selection
analysis that accounts for rare-group coverage. The existing development
experiments and the oracle theorem leave those questions open.
