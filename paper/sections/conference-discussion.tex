\section{Discussion}

Normalization changes how a finite training budget is distributed across
features. In the controlled binary setting, standardization can reduce
rare-feature bias while increasing propagated noise, and dependence can
make aggregate and rare-presence prediction favor different rules. The
same error moments explain both comparisons. Their interpretation requires
the rate, batch size, stopping count, and prediction objective to be stated
alongside the normalization rule.

The experiments also show why a fixed comparison cannot establish an
advantage for a tuned method. Both original anchor comparisons disappear
under the declared oracle adjustments. Estimated preprocessing and
validation selection retain relative-learning effects, but introduce
additional uncertainty from absent features, noisy selection, and the
search boundary. A rare-objective selection rule cannot directly assess
performance on rare observations that never appear in validation.

The scope remains limited to linear prediction, conventional diagonal
scalings, final iterates, and the stated finite searches. The population
theorems do not provide guarantees for estimated scales or selected
configurations. Fixed diagonal scaling is also distinct from the evolving
preconditioners of adaptive optimizers. A stronger normalization
recommendation would require a comparison that remains informative under
meaningful rate calibration and a selection analysis that accounts for
rare-group coverage. These are the principal unresolved steps beyond
the present comparisons.
