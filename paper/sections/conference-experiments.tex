\section{From calibrated comparisons to selected procedures}
\label{sec:conference-experiments}

We use three experimental settings to examine how the comparisons change with
the training and selection procedure. We first compute exact moments for SGD
with fresh observations, then sample with replacement from noisy training sets,
and finally evaluate predictions on held-out records from a categorical dataset.
The observation budget counts every sampled training row, including repeated
visits, and we select final iterates from stored checkpoints. These experiments
preceded Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} and motivated its formulation.
A prospective test of its predicted orderings remains open.

\subsection{Rate and stopping adjustments}

The exact-moment study covers 228 binary laws, 51,048 training configurations,
and 2,571,800 endpoints. We vary prevalence, dependence, signal, noise, learning
rate, batch size, and stopping, comparing both equal update counts and equal
observation counts. In the primary adjusted comparison, we select the
configuration with the lowest exact risk among mean-square stable candidates
and initialization. We use a coarse rate grid from
$1.953125 \times 10^{-6}$ to $.64$ and check its resolution separately on 16
laws. Selection uses the known test distribution, so this is an oracle
comparison.

Both original anchor comparisons disappear after these adjustments.
Table~\ref{tab:conference-adjustment} shows the rare-risk differences at the
independent anchor of Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c1}, before and after selecting
the rate and stopping count. S no longer has higher risk at the late $B = 1$
stop, with selected rates $h_U = .004$ and $h_S = .0003125$. At the dependent
anchor of Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-c2}, selection favors U under both
objectives. Reversals and disagreements between objectives remain for other
laws. These results illustrate why Theorems~\ref{thm:conference-c1}
and~\ref{thm:conference-c2} apply to the fixed calibration. The separate oracle
comparison in Theorem~\ref{thm:conference-predictive} establishes orderings that
persist after optimizing over all constant rates and deterministic stops on its
two signal families.

We retain invalid candidates in the unrestricted reports. Overflow and invalid
moment calculations leave some of those comparisons unresolved. In one case
dominated by extreme outcomes, a paired Monte Carlo covariance diagnostic also
exceeds its declared tolerance. We report this failure together with a further
investigation conditional on rare-feature absence. The exact comparisons among
stable candidates remain available, but these diagnostics prevent a claim of
complete agreement with simulation.

\subsection{Estimated preprocessing and validation selection}

We next use eight binary laws with 128 independent replications per law,
$n = 256$ training observations, and 128 validation observations. We compare
known preprocessing, estimated scales, training centering with a fixed or fitted
intercept, and uncentered predictors with a fitted intercept. The feature
divisors are $1$ for no scaling, $\sqrt{v_j}$ for SD scaling, and
$\sqrt{v_j + 0.01}$ for damped scaling \eqref{eq:conference-damped}. Estimated
variances use denominator $n$. Constant training columns are frozen at zero
coefficient with divisor one. Fitted intercepts start at the training response
mean. Appendix~\ref{sec:practical} gives further protocol details.

Each scaling and preprocessing arm receives the same five rate factors
\[
 \eta = \frac{a_0}{\max(L,10^{-12})},\qquad a_0 \in \{.001,.01,.05,.2,.8\},
\]
where $L$ is the largest empirical curvature, including the intercept coordinate
when fitted. This calibration does not guarantee stochastic stability. We use
batches $B \in \{1,16\}$ and observation caps $M \in \{128,512,2048\}$. We
compare fixed $a_0 = .05$, $B = 16$ with validation selection of the rate and
batch size, or of the rate, batch size, and stopping count. Initialization is
also eligible. The ten paths for each preprocessing arm and scaling rule cost
$10M$ processed observations at cap $M$, with checkpoints sharing paths. We use
no test outcomes during selection and do not refit the selected models.

For the independent equal-signal law at $q = .02$ and $\sigma^2 = 3$, centering
the predictors and fitting the intercept gives fixed-configuration rare-risk
differences of $-.1653$ (paired MCSE $.0295$) at $M = 128$ and $+.0901$
($.0697$) at $M = 2048$. The early advantage is clear, while the late
disadvantage is within two MCSE of zero. At the latter budget, the rare-only
signal favors S by $.2320$
($.0233$), while the common-only signal favors U by $.1272$ ($.0159$). These
sampled-data results use empirical rate calibration, so they do not extend the
exact population theorems. Figure~\ref{fig:conference-practical} shows the fixed
paths and the categorical-task comparisons described below.

\paragraph{When validation contains no rare cases.}

At $q = .002$, the two anchor laws have no rare validation observations in 106
and 105 of 128 replications. In these cases, the declared selection rule falls
back to aggregate loss. One selected SD fit with centered predictors has a rare
coefficient of about $-6.719 \times 10^{16}$ and validation half squared loss
$2.086$, yet its exact rare loss over the binary cells is about
$2.257 \times 10^{33}$. To investigate numerical error, we replay all eligible
trajectories in the worst selected replication for each of 24 combinations of
law and scaling, using 160-digit decimal arithmetic. All 24 selections remain
unchanged, so the detected floating-point cancellation does not explain this
failure. We retain the extreme finite outcomes and 151 failed synthetic
endpoints in the archive. Means dominated by these extremes do not precisely
estimate population risk.

\subsection{A sparse categorical prediction task}

We use squared loss and all 22 categorical predictors to predict the recorded
poisonous label in UCI Mushroom \citep{uciMushroom}. The declared subgroup
consists of mushrooms with green spore-print color, which accounts for 72 of
8124 records. We construct the one-hot vocabulary and estimate means and scales
using training data alone. We treat missingness as a category and map unknown
categories to zero blocks. We store the design sparsely and implement centering
algebraically. Coefficients are reported on the original feature scale, but
depend on the optimizer because the full one-hot design is rank deficient. We
do not clip predictions.

We use 32 random partitions at each pair of training and validation sizes,
$(256,128)$ and $(2048,1024)$, reserving the remaining records for testing. All
candidates share partitions and replacement index streams. Each scaling
receives the same five rate factors and $B \in \{16,128\}$, with
$M \in \{512,2048,8192\}$. In this frozen study, aggregate validation selects
the upper rate factor $.8$ for unscaled training in all 32 large-sample
partitions. We therefore declare a separate follow-up with 17 factors from
$.001$ to $6.4$ for every scaling and preprocessing arm, keeping the same
splits, index streams, and stopping checkpoints. Each scaling now uses 34
trajectories, costing $34M$ observations at cap $M$. A further declared
comparison adds variance scaling, $s_j = v_j$, and gives all four rules 25
factors from $10^{-5}$ to $409.6$, at a cost of $50M$ per rule.
Table~\ref{tab:conference-mushroom} reports the results of this four-rule
comparison. Appendices~\ref{sec:mushroom-rate-search}
and~\ref{sec:variance-scaling-followup} describe the retained paths and failures,
comparisons with the earlier searches, and separate checks of stopping
resolution.

Expanding the rate search reduces rare loss from $.012482$ to $.005955$ for
unscaled training selected by aggregate validation. The SD-minus-none rare-risk
gap shrinks from $-.006570$ (paired MCSE $.001526$) to $-.001192$ ($.001318$),
which is within two MCSE of zero. We can therefore no longer resolve the
original SD advantage, although this does not establish equivalence. Damping
retains lower aggregate loss, with a difference of $-.000532$ ($.000056$), and
lower rare loss, with a difference of $-.005273$ ($.000797$). When selection
uses rare validation loss, SD retains a rare-risk advantage of $-.001421$
($.000470$), but its aggregate loss varies widely, with median $.004357$ and
maximum $2.36513$.

Variance scaling has higher rare loss than no scaling, with a difference of
$.045630$ (paired MCSE $.008323$). The aggregate difference of $.310647$
($.201754$) is unresolved under the two-MCSE convention, with aggregate median
$.069802$ and maximum $6.505207$. With the original stopping checkpoints, the
wider grid leaves every selected Mushroom predictor unchanged from the nested
17-factor search. Every selection with a positive budget is now away from both
rate boundaries, including in the refined stopping search. These comparisons
resolve the boundaries within the tested grid but do not establish continuous
optimality.

All 32 unscaled aggregate selections now use interior factors $.6$--$1.8$, and
the next higher tested factors give worse validation scores. The mean green
coefficients after expansion are $.5999$, $.7763$, and $.9126$ for none, SD,
and damping, with other features partly offsetting these contributions. Across
both sizes and all arms, the 17-factor study retains 2950 failed endpoints.
Its secondary search with uncentered predictors and rare validation selected the
upper rate boundary when extra early stops were available. The four-rule
extension resolves that boundary within the grid. At the smaller sample size,
five training partitions and thirteen validation partitions contain no green
observations. MCSEs describe variation across partitions and optimization runs
within this dataset. Because the records are hypothetical descriptions derived
from a field guide, performance on random partitions does not establish
generalization to new species or field data.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the damping offset.}

In a separate comparison, declared before evaluating its candidates, we test
$s_j = \sqrt{v_j + \tau}$ at five fixed positive offsets with centered
predictors and a fitted intercept. Each rule receives the same rate, batch, and
stopping searches. At $n = 2048$, $M = 8192$, and aggregate validation, offsets
$.003$, $.01$, $.03$, and $.1$ improve both risks over no scaling by more than
two paired MCSE. At $.001$, the aggregate improvement is unresolved. At
$n = 256$, rare validation selects an extreme finite path at $\tau = .03$,
showing that damping does not prevent selection failures. This study also
includes variance scaling, whose rate boundary is addressed by the four-rule
extension above. We give the full results and limitations in
Appendix~\ref{sec:mushroom-sensitivity}.
