\section{Introduction}

Feature normalization is part of the learning procedure. Dividing a predictor
by its standard deviation changes how quickly gradient methods learn its
coefficient and how strongly individual observations perturb that coefficient.
Under a finite training budget, these changes can affect the predictor even
when the unregularized least-squares solution is invariant to scaling. The
statistical question is therefore how normalization changes prediction at the
available budget, and which effects remain after calibrating the other
training choices.

Rare features make this question concrete. Without scaling, a rare binary
feature has small curvature and can remain poorly learned when common features
are already fitted. Standardization increases its learning rate relative to
common features. It also magnifies the stochastic contribution of the few
observations on which the feature is present. Early stopping and batch size
determine the balance between those effects. Dependence adds a further issue:
prediction for a rare subgroup depends on joint coefficient errors, so the
ordering of individual coefficient errors need not determine the ordering of
subgroup prediction risks.

We analyze squared-loss linear regression with a common and a rare binary
predictor. The first comparisons use known population centering and scales,
zero initialization, a fixed intercept, and fresh observations at each update.
An exact second-moment calculation yields two uniform comparisons. First,
standard-deviation scaling has lower rare-presence risk at an early stop and
higher risk later under a specified rate calibration; a larger batch changes
that ordering. Second, the same pair of stable training procedures can favor
opposite normalization rules under aggregate and rare-presence risk, even
when scaling increases both individual coefficient mean squared errors.

The experiments examine the limits of these comparisons. A broader search
over rates and stopping removes both original anchor comparisons, although
other laws retain reversals or objective disagreements. Sampled-data
experiments then replace population preprocessing by estimated centering and
scales, fit an intercept, and select configurations on validation data. A
sparse categorical task shows a rare-subgroup benefit within the declared
search, alongside rate-boundary selections and a tradeoff with aggregate
prediction. Missing rare validation observations can conceal extreme subgroup
error. These findings motivate evaluating normalization as part of the full
training and selection procedure.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}
The connection between preconditioning and statistical performance is
established \citep{amari2020preconditioning,su2024preconditioning}. Early
stopping changes least-squares regularization \citep{ali2019early};
least-mean-squares analyses already separate bias and variance and study
sampling and batching \citep{defossez2015averaged,jain2018parallelizing}.
AdaBatch relates sparse mini-batch aggregation to coordinate rescaling
\citep{defossez2017adabatch}, and exact mean-square stability can be expressed
through moment operators \citep{mulayoff2024exact}. Target-aware
preconditioning under covariate shift also studies the dependence on the test
objective \citep{liu2025precondition}. For explicit regularization,
\citet{larsson2025normalization} analyze the influence of feature scaling on
shrinkage. Our comparisons specialize these broader mechanisms to conventional
normalization, final iterates, original-scale coefficients, and rare-presence
prediction. Neither diagonal preconditioning nor the existence of a
bias--variance tradeoff is a new mechanism here. The question is what
quantitative comparisons they yield, and how those comparisons change when
the entire training procedure is tuned.
