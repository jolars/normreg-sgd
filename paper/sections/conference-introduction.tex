\section{Introduction}

Dividing a predictor by its standard deviation changes both how quickly gradient
methods learn its coefficient and how strongly individual observations affect
that coefficient. Although scaling leaves the full-rank least-squares solution
unchanged, it can therefore affect predictions when training stops
before convergence. In this paper, we study how normalization affects prediction
under a finite training budget and whether these effects persist when we tune
the learning rate and stopping time.

Rare binary features provide a simple setting in which to study this question.
Without scaling, the loss has little curvature in the rare coordinate, so its
coefficient can remain poorly learned after the common coefficients have
already been fitted. Standardization increases its learning rate relative to
the common features, but also amplifies the contribution of the few observations
on which it is present. The balance depends on when we stop training and how
many observations we use in each update. When features are dependent, their
coefficient errors also contribute jointly to prediction. Comparing individual
coefficient errors can then give a different ordering from comparing prediction
risks for the rare subgroup.

We consider linear regression with squared loss, a common binary predictor, and
a rare binary predictor. In the population comparisons, we use known centering
and scales, initialize the coefficients at zero, fix the intercept, and draw
fresh observations at each update. Our main result compares the lowest risk
that each normalization can attain on observations where the rare feature is
present. We allow each method to choose any nonnegative constant learning rate
and any deterministic stop within the same observation budget. Standardization
has lower risk on a specified family in which the rare signal dominates, whereas
unscaled training has lower risk on a family in which the common signal
dominates. Both gaps remain bounded away from zero as prevalence decreases with
the expected number of rare training observations held of order one. The result
therefore shows that separate tuning need not remove the effect of relative
scaling. It applies to two independent features and one observation per update;
learning-rate schedules and data-dependent selection rules fall outside its
scope.

We also examine how these effects arise along particular training paths. Under
a fixed rate calibration, standardization gives lower rare-presence risk early
in training and higher risk later, and increasing the batch size changes this
ordering. With dependent features, standardization can improve rare-presence
prediction while increasing both aggregate risk and the mean squared error of
each coefficient.

Our experiments motivated the main theorem and show why the training and
selection procedure matters. Searching over more rates and stopping times
removes both original anchor comparisons, although reversals and disagreements
between objectives remain for other laws. We then replace population
preprocessing with estimated centering and scales, fit an intercept, and use
validation data to select configurations. On a sparse categorical task, we find
a rare-subgroup benefit within the declared search, together with selections at
the rate boundary and a tradeoff with aggregate prediction. We also find that
missing rare validation observations can conceal extreme subgroup errors. These
experiments preceded the theorem, so a prospective test of its predicted
orderings remains to be conducted.

\paragraph{Relation to prior work.}

Several authors have studied how preconditioning affects statistical
performance \citep{amari2020preconditioning, su2024preconditioning}. In
particular, \citet[Section 4.2, Proposition 7]{amari2020preconditioning} show
that the preferred preconditioner depends on signal alignment even after
optimizing early-stopping bias in an overparameterized model with fixed data.
We instead compare total rare-subgroup risk, including variance from stochastic
updates, in a fixed-dimensional model with fresh observations. Under the same
observation cap, we obtain explicit gaps between the best risks attainable over
all constant rates and deterministic stops.

Early stopping also changes least-squares regularization \citep{ali2019early},
and analyses of least-mean-squares methods separate bias and variance while
accounting for sampling and batching \citep{defossez2015averaged,
jain2018parallelizing}. AdaBatch connects sparse mini-batch aggregation with
coordinate rescaling \citep{defossez2017adabatch}, while moment operators
characterize exact mean-square stability \citep{mulayoff2024exact}.
\citet{liu2025precondition} study how the test objective affects preconditioning
under covariate shift. For explicit regularization,
\citet{larsson2025normalization} analyze how feature scaling affects shrinkage.
We build on these mechanisms to compare conventional normalization rules using
final iterates, coefficients on the original scale, and rare-presence risk. Our
contribution is the quantitative comparison and its dependence on tuning;
diagonal preconditioning and the bias--variance tradeoff are established
mechanisms.
